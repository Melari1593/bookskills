# Chapter 3: Infusions to Soothe Your Aches & Pains

## Core Idea
Topical pain relief in this book is almost entirely the **salve build from ch01** (infused oil → double boiler → beeswax → optional butter) applied to a rotating cast of anti-inflammatory herbs (arnica, Saint John's wort, cannabis, lavender/peppermint), while internal pain relief comes from a small set of anti-inflammatory teas/decoctions (white willow, turmeric).

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Decarboxylation via stovetop infusion**: for cannabis specifically, low, slow heat (under 150°F/65°C, 4–8 hours) both infuses the oil *and* converts the plant's raw compounds into their bioavailable, medicinally active forms — this is different from the ch01 cold-infusion default because heat is a functional requirement, not just a shortcut.
- **Bioavailability pairing**: turmeric's curcumin is poorly absorbed alone; black pepper's piperine makes it bioavailable. Apply this "pairing herb" logic whenever a recipe's target compound has known absorption problems.
- **Any infused oil → optional salve**: nearly every recipe in this chapter notes it can be left as a rub-on oil *or* converted into a salve by melting in beeswax (roughly ½–1 oz per ½ cup of infused oil) — reinforcing that the oil is the platform and the salve is just one downstream option (see [ch01](ch01-basics.md)).

## Key Concepts
- **Arnica**: topical-only anti-inflammatory; **toxic if ingested** — never used internally, store out of reach of children/pets.
- **Salicylic acid herbs**: white willow bark and birch bark both contain natural aspirin precursors; the tea has a mild blood-thinning effect like aspirin and should be limited to 1–2 weeks of use.
- **THC vs. CBD**: THC is psychoactive: ("stoned" feeling); CBD is pain-relieving with minimal psychoactivity — choose strain by desired effect, check local legality.
- **Four-Herb Wound Salve logic**: each herb contributes a distinct wound-healing action — yarrow stops bleeding, plantain soothes/prevents infection, comfrey grows new skin cells, calendula speeds healing and reduces scarring. This is the book's clearest example of combining herbs by *complementary mechanism* rather than redundancy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a burn with an oil-based salve**: oil seals in heat and worsens burns — use a honey-based ointment instead (see Herbal Honey Burn Ointment).
- **Ingesting arnica**: topical-only; toxic in any internal amount.
- **Exceeding 1–2 weeks on white willow/birch bark tea**: mild blood-thinning effect compounds with extended use — treat as a short-course remedy, not a daily tonic.
- **Confusing coniferous hemlock with poison hemlock**: applies more broadly in the book (ch04's Pine Needle Cough Syrup), but the general caution — verify plant ID before foraging look-alikes — belongs here as a running theme.

## Worked Example
**Four-Herb Wound Salve**, reconstructed to show the complementary-mechanism pattern: Infused oil — 2 tbsp each dried calendula, yarrow, plantain, comfrey leaf steeped 4–6 weeks in ¼ cup each melted coconut oil, olive oil, sweet almond oil. Salve — strain, measure ½ cup of the infused oil, melt with ½ oz beeswax in a double boiler, pour, cool 3–4 hrs. Use: apply to minor cuts/scrapes/bruises/burns/blemishes/scars as needed. Safe for ages 13+ after patch test; under 13, substitute [Boo-Boo Balm](ch08-mother-child.md) instead (gentler formulation, no comfrey). The herb selection itself is the lesson: rather than four herbs doing the same thing, each targets a different stage of wound healing (bleeding → infection → new skin growth → scar reduction), so the combination outperforms any single herb.

## Key Takeaways
1. Nearly all topical pain relief here is the ch01 salve build; the differentiator is herb selection, not technique.
2. Cannabis is the exception to the cold-infusion default — heat is required for decarboxylation, not just speed.
3. Pair curcumin (turmeric) with piperine (black pepper) for bioavailability; this "pairing" pattern is worth applying elsewhere.
4. Never treat a burn with an oil-based product — use honey-based instead.
5. Build multi-herb wound formulas around complementary actions (stop bleeding / prevent infection / regrow skin / reduce scarring), not redundant ones.
6. Salicylate-containing teas (white willow, birch bark) carry the same time-limits and precautions as aspirin.
