# Chapter 2: Infusions to Boost Your Immunity

## Core Idea
Immune support draws on a small recurring cast of herbs — elderberry, echinacea, garlic, ginger, oregano, rose hips — delivered through whichever medium fits the moment: a fast tea for acute onset, a fermented honey for a slow-build daily tonic, or a tincture for a strong 5-year-shelf-stable reserve.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Fermented Honey Infusion**: a honey infusion made with *fresh, watery* plant material (garlic, onion, ginger) deliberately ferments because the herb's moisture "wakes up" the honey's wild yeast.
  - How: combine chopped fresh herb + raw honey in a jar, invert several times, loosen the lid to vent gas, turn/tighten/loosen daily for 1–2 weeks, ferment ~1 month in a cool dark place; usable throughout the process, not just at the end.
  - Why it works: raw honey is unpasteurized and full of wild yeast; only *dried* herbs skip fermentation because they lack the water to trigger it. This is a deliberate application of the "fresh herbs are safe in high-sugar media" rule from [ch01](ch01-basics.md).

- **Preventative-dose vs. onset-dose pattern**: nearly every immunity recipe has two dosing modes — a lighter daily/preventative dose through cold-and-flu season, and a heavier, more frequent dose (up to 3x/day) at first sign of illness.

## Key Concepts
- **Oxymel ratio**: 1:1 honey to vinegar is the author's preferred balance — "a little sweet, a little sour."
- **"Fiery cider" / immune vinegar**: garlic, ginger, turmeric, oregano, thyme, citrus infused in apple cider vinegar; hot peppers are optional, not required for efficacy.
- **Astragalus**: TCM adaptogen root, gentle immune stimulant, safe for the whole family, often added to broths.
- **Echinacea**: strongest when taken at the *earliest* sign of illness — timing matters more than dose.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using dried herbs when a fermented honey is wanted**: dried herbs lack the moisture to trigger fermentation — use fresh for that effect, dried if you specifically want a non-fermenting infused honey instead.
- **Skipping the "earliest sign" window for echinacea/elderberry**: these herbs are timing-sensitive; starting them after illness is established blunts their effectiveness relative to starting at the first symptom.

## Worked Example
**Fresh Kitchen Herb Oxymel** — chosen as the worked example because it shows the oxymel pattern generalized to whatever's on hand. Ingredients: 1 cup loosely packed fresh garden herbs (rosemary, thyme, sage, basil, oregano, mint), ¾ cup raw apple cider vinegar, ¾ cup raw honey. Method: combine all three in a pint jar, shake to combine honey and vinegar thoroughly, infuse in a cool dark place 4–6 weeks, strain. Dose: 1 tbsp up to 3x/day at illness onset, 1–2x/day as a season-long preventative; safe for children 2+ at guideline doses (see [cheatsheet.md](../cheatsheet.md)). The pattern (equal parts vinegar and honey + whatever culinary herbs are in season) is reusable for any immune-support blend without a fixed recipe.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fresh vs. dried herb choice in a honey infusion is a deliberate lever: fresh → ferments into a "live" tonic, dried → stays a stable infused honey.
2. Immunity recipes generally have two doses: light/daily-preventative and heavy/onset — apply the heavier dose only at first symptoms.
3. Echinacea and elderberry are most effective started at the earliest sign of illness, not after symptoms build.
4. The oxymel ratio (1:1 honey:vinegar) and the fiery-cider template (aromatics + acid) both generalize beyond the book's exact recipes.
5. Oregano-Infused Oil with Lemon is the sole fresh-herb oil recipe in the book — a live demonstration of the ch01 fresh-oil exception (acid addition + short use window).
