# Chapter 11: General Advice (Appendixes)

Source: FERRANDI Paris, *Fruits & Nuts*, Appendixes > General Advice (book pp. 298-299). Short reference entries on baking-adjacent staples used throughout the book's recipes.

## Bain-Marie
**For**: Gently heating delicate mixtures (chocolate, egg-based creams/sauces) that would separate or burn over direct heat.
**Method**: A heatproof bowl set over a saucepan of barely simmering water, with the bowl's base not touching the water (a double boiler works the same way). Also used in the oven — a water bath around flans/custards while baking prevents the eggs from curdling and keeps the top from drying and cracking, via slow, even heat.

## Eggs
Recipes give egg quantities by weight, volume, and approximate whole-egg count. Unless stated otherwise, quantities assume a "large" egg (US/Canada) or "medium" egg (UK) — about 2 oz (55 g) in-shell. Take eggs out of the refrigerator ~30 minutes before using so they reach room temperature and match the temperature of other ingredients (important for emulsification in creams, mousses, and batters).

## Butter
Unless a recipe says otherwise, use unsalted butter with **at least 82% butterfat**. Higher fat content means less water, which produces richer creams, sauces, ganaches, and flakier crusts. Laminated doughs (puff pastry, croissant, brioche feuilletée) specifically call for **84% butterfat** butter. For "softened" butter, pull it from the fridge at least 30 minutes ahead.

## Fresh Yeast
Found refrigerated at larger supermarkets, or from a bakery/pizzeria. Substitute: **50% of the weight** in active dry yeast, or **40% of the weight** in instant yeast (follow the package's usage instructions for either).

## Gelatin
Sheet and powdered gelatin are generally interchangeable once hydrated. Both must be fully hydrated in cold water, then fully dissolved into a warm liquid **no hotter than 158°F/70°C** (higher heat damages its setting power).
- **Sheet gelatin**: soak in a bowl of cold water (the sheet only absorbs what it needs, so the water quantity isn't critical) for ~5 minutes until soft, then squeeze out excess water before adding to a hot-but-not-boiling mixture, stirring until dissolved. This book uses **200 Bloom gold-strength sheets, ~1/14 oz (2 g) each**.
- **Powdered gelatin**: sprinkle over the recipe's specified quantity of cold water, let soak ~10 minutes until swollen, then stir into a hot liquid until dissolved.
- **Plant-based alternatives**: agar-agar, carrageenan, and vegan jel are usable substitutes where a recipe calls for them.

## Cream
Unless stated otherwise, use cream with a minimum of **35% fat**. Higher-fat creams whip more reliably and are less likely to curdle when added to hot mixtures. In the US, look for "heavy cream" or "heavy whipping cream"; in the UK, double or whipping cream.

## Degrees Brix and Refractometers
**Degrees Brix (°Brix)** measures the sugar/sweetness content of a liquid, read with a **refractometer**. Not essential, but very useful for fruit-based preparations (jams, jellies, syrups, ice creams, sorbets) where consistent sugar concentration is what makes the recipe set/freeze/preserve correctly.
Key reference: **1° Brix = 1% sugar** in the liquid (a reading of 10° = 10% sugar).
For jam-making specifically, a **final sugar content of 60-65%** (including both natural and added sugar) is the target — high enough to inhibit microbial growth and give good shelf life, without tasting cloying.
For recipes in this book, a refractometer with a **0-80% Brix range** is recommended, used per the manufacturer's instructions.

## Measurements
Weigh ingredients on a digital scale (preferably in metric units) for the most reliable results — cup and spoon measures in this book are rounded for practicality and are less precise. When using cups/spoons, measures should be **level** unless a recipe says otherwise.

## How This Chapter Connects to the Rest of the Book
