## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**No mind/body split**: TCM never separates mental from physical symptoms — every diagnosis is a *pattern* combining both (e.g., Liver Qi Stagnation = irritability/depression + irregular bowels + menstrual irregularity). Always read the full physical symptom picture (temperature of extremities, digestion, pulse) alongside the mental complaint before selecting treatment.

**Qi and the Five Organs**: Mental illness stems from qi (vital energy) being depleted, blocked, or misdirected among five metaphorical Organ systems — Heart, Lung, Spleen, Kidney, Liver (not literal anatomy). The Heart is the seat of awareness; all emotion is processed through it.

**Five Phases / Five Emotions correspondence** — the master diagnostic lookup table:
| Emotion | Organ | Direction | Qi Movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anger | Liver | East/Wood | Rising |
| Joy | Heart | South/Fire | Relaxing |
| Sadness | Lung | West/Metal | Dissipating |
| Fear | Kidney | North/Water | Descending |
| Fright | Heart-Kidney | — | Chaos/panic |
| Pensiveness | Spleen | Center/Earth | Lumping/dampening |

**"One formula, many patterns"**: A single herbal formula (e.g., Xiao Yao San family) can treat several seemingly unrelated Western diagnoses if they share a TCM pattern. Conversely, one Western diagnosis can map to multiple different TCM patterns depending on the individual's full presentation. Always diagnose the *pattern*, not the label.

**Depression vs. Anxiety — energy direction**: Depression = exhausted/immobilized qi ("stuck," an irrepressible force meeting an immovable object). Anxiety = a rush of uncontrolled qi. Same stagnation family (Yu, 郁), opposite direction. This single heuristic resolves most differential-diagnosis confusion between the two.

**Jun-Chen-Zuo-Shi formula architecture** ("Emperor-Minister-Assistant-Courier"): every well-designed formula has a main herb (Jun), an adjuvant that boosts its effect (Chen), a side-effect reducer (Zuo), and an Organ-guiding herb (Shi). This is why formulas use 2–10+ herbs instead of one "active ingredient" — isolating a single herb from its formula context (e.g., Ma Huang → Ephedra) breaks the built-in synergy/safety design and has historically caused serious harm. *Evidence note: support for this design comes from computer models and rat studies, not human trials, and combination does not guarantee safety (aristolochic acid products, Taiwan) — see [evidence.md](evidence.md) 2.2, 2.12.*

**Formula-taking protocol**: warm water only (never juice/coffee — alters pharmacology); space ≥1 hour from Western medications; full dose for 4–6 weeks; expect improvement within 1–2 weeks or reassess the pattern match; taper once symptoms resolve. *Evidence note: no study shows that a 1-hour gap prevents herb–drug interactions; metabolic (CYP) effects last much longer. Check interactions with a pharmacist or physician instead of relying on spacing — [evidence.md](evidence.md) 2.1.*

**TCM Body Clock**: optimal bedtime 9–11 p.m.; be fully asleep by 11 p.m.–1 a.m. (the book cites chronobiology as corroboration, but heart attacks and sudden cardiac death peak 6 a.m.–noon, maximum ~9 a.m. — not 11 a.m.–1 p.m. — so the organ clock is not corroborated; circadian disruption and metabolic risk is a separate, real research area — [evidence.md](evidence.md) 2.4).

**Evidence base (as the book presents it, with corrections)**: The book says TCM has been tested in placebo-controlled trials since the 1960s and that the Xiao Yao San family matched or outperformed SSRIs/benzodiazepines with faster onset and no adverse effects. The evidence check found: many trials exist and several point in a favorable direction (e.g., Suan Zao Ren Tang vs. lorazepam, tui na for insomnia), but most are small, unblinded and low certainty (only 3 % of 3,159 TCM trials reported adequate randomization, concealment and blinding); the double-blind sertraline comparison used *Jia Wei* Xiao Yao capsules in 2019 with *similar* adverse events in both arms; and the 1,837-patient review says the trials *did not report* adverse effects. "Traditional" does not mean "untested", and "tested" does not mean "proven" — see [evidence.md](evidence.md) 1.1, 1.3–1.5.

**Multi-modal treatment**: Every condition chapter offers herbal formulas *plus* acupressure, Five Element music therapy, food therapy, qigong/daoyin, and (for some) feng shui — treatment is rarely herb-only.

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the book's content only — a synthesis of Nina Cheng's presentation of TCM mental health frameworks, not an independent medical reference. Herbal formulas listed here have specific contraindications (see cheatsheet.md) and dosing protocols; treat this as a study/reference tool, not a substitute for a licensed TCM practitioner or physician. For topics beyond this book, ask directly or consult other sources. Several of the book's safety statements are incomplete: classical Tian Wang Bu Xin Dan contains cinnabar (mercury), Yunnan Baiyao contains aconite, long-term Gardenia-containing formulas (e.g., Jia Wei Xiao Yao San) are linked to mesenteric phlebosclerosis on Japanese labels, ginseng can interact with warfarin, and Huang Lian (berberine) should be avoided in jaundiced newborns — see [evidence.md](evidence.md) before recommending any formula.
