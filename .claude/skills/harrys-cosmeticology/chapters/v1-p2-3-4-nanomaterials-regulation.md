# Part 2.3.4: Nanomaterials in Cosmetics – Regulatory and Safety Considerations
**Authors**: Jeffrey W. Card and Tomas Jonaitis (Intertek Cantox)

## Core Idea
Nanomaterial definitions and rules differ between Europe, the US and Canada, with the EU the first and most prescriptive regulator (6-month pre-market notification, "[nano]" labeling, public catalogue). Safety assessment of nanomaterials needs substance-specific data, rigorous physicochemical characterization in several states, alternative dose metrics (surface area, particle number) and awareness that nanoparticles can interfere with standard in vitro assays. Regulation and science are both still evolving.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **EU nano notification (Reg. 1223/2009)**: notify 6 months before marketing (vs immediately before for non-nano products).
  - When to use: any EU product with an ingredient meeting the nano definition.
  - How: submit (1) identity incl. IUPAC name; (2) specification incl. particle size and physico-chemical properties; (3) estimated tonnage per year; (4) toxicological profile; (5) safety data for the product category; (6) reasonably foreseeable exposure. Commission may ask SCCS for an opinion with set timelines; opinions are public.
- **SCCS characterization parameters (16)**: chemical identity; composition and purity; size and distribution; morphology; surface characteristics (charge, modifications); solubility; surface area; catalytic activity; concentration; dustiness (dry powders); density and pore density; redox potential; pH; viscosity; stability; other (UV absorption, light reflection).
- **Three-state characterization (SCCS)**: characterize, unless justified otherwise, (i) as manufactured (raw), (ii) in the final formulation, (iii) as dosed in toxicology tests (dose formulation, cell culture).
- **FDA safety-assessment factors (2012 draft guidance)**: physicochemical properties (size/distribution, aggregation/agglomeration, surface chemistry, morphology, solubility, density, stability, porosity); agglomeration under test conditions matching the final product; impurities; exposure and agglomeration in final product; dosimetry for in vitro/in vivo; tox data (dermal penetration, skin/eye irritation, sensitization, mutagenicity/genotoxicity); clinical studies.
  - Minimum FDA test battery: acute toxicity, skin irritation, dermal photo-irritation, skin sensitization, mutagenicity/genotoxicity, repeat-dose toxicity (21–28 days), subchronic toxicity (90 days), with toxicokinetics/toxicodynamics by route; adapt solvents, dosing formulations, anti-agglomeration methods, purity and stability conditions.
- **Tiered testing strategy**: in vitro and in vivo screening → pivotal in vivo studies → supportive mechanistic studies; ideally OECD guidelines and GLP.

## Key Concepts
- **EU definition**: insoluble or biopersistent, intentionally manufactured material with one or more external dimensions, or an internal structure, at 1–100 nm.
- **US**: FDA has no formal definition; term commonly means intentional manipulation/manufacture/selection of materials with at least one dimension ~1–100 nm; one definition may be too narrow or broad in another context.
- **Canada (Health Canada 2011 working definition)**: manufactured substance at/within nanoscale (1–100 nm inclusive) in at least one external dimension or with nanoscale internal/surface structure, OR larger/smaller in all dimensions but showing nanoscale properties/phenomena; "manufactured" includes engineering processes and control of matter.
- **EU labeling and catalogue**: "(nano)" after ingredient name; public catalogue of nanomaterials in cosmetics by January 11, 2014; first review of nano provisions by July 11, 2018.
- **US status**: no notification or premarket approval for cosmetics or ingredients (except color additives); manufacturer must ensure no adulteration/misbranding and hold safety data before marketing; FDA invites early consultation for new or nanoscale versions of existing ingredients.
- **Canada status**: Cosmetic Notification Form within 10 days of first sale (company contacts, purpose, form, ingredients and concentrations); Cosmetic Ingredient Hotlist (no nanomaterials listed as of late 2012; not exhaustive); Food and Drugs Act Section 16 allows restricting any unsafe ingredient; Health Canada may request nano data (intended use, manufacturing, properties a–n, tox/ecotox/fate data, risk management).
- **CEPA NSNR**: cosmetic ingredients not on the Domestic Substances List (DSL) need New Substances Notification; volume-based Schedules depend on kg/year, substance type (chemical, polymer, low-concern polymer), use (R&D, site-limited, export-only) and NDSL listing. Nanoforms of DSL substances are "new" only if they have unique structures or molecular arrangements; incidental or naturally occurring nanomaterials are exempt. (Minister's intention for new environmental legislation for Food and Drugs Act products dated September 13, 2001.)
- **No read-across yet**: data are insufficient for read-across/categories; substance-specific data generally required; literature usable only if the material is physico-chemically similar and studies are of adequate quality.
- **Literature quality gap**: many published nano studies omit animals per group, route or doses; over half of oral-toxicity papers on food-related nanomaterials lacked purity information.
- **EU testing difficulty**: animal-testing restrictions plus validated alternatives (skin corrosion, irritation, mutagenicity, photomutagenicity, phototoxicity, dermal absorption) not validated for nanomaterials.
- **Dose metrics**: mass/volume (mg/kg bw, mg/L) may mislead for insoluble nanomaterials; particle number or surface area may be more relevant. Oberdörster: rat lung inflammation steeper for 25 nm than 250 nm TiO2 per mass, but curves overlapped when expressed per surface area.
- **"Beauty from within"**: growth of nutraceuticals raises the importance of oral nano toxicity, since nanoparticles may penetrate cells.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Build the nano dossier early: EU notification lead time is 6 months; label "(nano)".
- Characterize the material raw, in formulation and in test media; report purity.
- Design tox studies to allow surface-area and particle-number dose metrics; control agglomeration in dosing vehicles.
- Screen in vitro results for assay interference (optical/fluorescent readout interference, ROS assay interference, adsorption of nutrients/growth factors causing apparent cytotoxicity); MTT, recommended in OECD TG 431 (in vitro skin corrosion), can give false positives.
- In Canada, check DSL status of the nanoform and notify under NSNR if new; contact Health Canada early.
- In the US, consult FDA when introducing new nanomaterials or nanoscale versions of marketed ingredients.

## Mental Models
- Same chemistry, different size = potentially different substance; treat nanoforms as their own materials.
- Dose is about surface, not just mass, for insoluble particles.
- A nanomaterial can fool the assay as well as the cell; verify the readout before trusting the toxicity result.

## Anti-patterns
- **Read-across from bulk or other nanomaterials**: insufficient data to justify it.
- **Citing poorly characterized literature**: missing purity, dose or route data make studies unusable for safety assessment.
- **Characterizing only the raw powder**: properties change in formulation and test media.
- **Expressing dose only as mg/kg**: can misrepresent dose–response for nanoparticles.
