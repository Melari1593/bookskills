# Part 4.2.1: Natural and Synthetic Polymers: Designing Rheological Properties for Applications
**Authors**: Susan Freers (Grain Processing Corporation)

## Core Idea
Rheological additives do far more than set viscosity: they suspend particles, stabilize emulsions, build foam, form films, add lubricity, condition, and shape appearance and texture. Choosing among the many workable options depends less on label/price and more on the stresses the formula meets: pH, processing temperature and shear, packaging/dispensing, and consumer handling. Additives split into those that modify water (aqueous) and those that modify non-water liquids (anhydrous), though emulsions can draw on both.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Aqueous vs. non-aqueous classification**: water-phase modifiers (natural gums and derivatives, cellulosics, starch derivatives, clays, PEGs, fatty alcohols, synthetic polymers) vs. oil/anhydrous modifiers (silicas, polyethylenes, fatty acids, castor oil derivatives, aluminum magnesium hydroxide stearate, stearic acid derivatives, organoclay pre-dispersions).
  - When to use: first cut when shortlisting thickeners.
  - How: identify which phase needs structure; remember PEGs and synthetic polymers work in either.
- **Shear-response design (thixotropic vs. pseudoplastic)**: match recovery behavior to use and process.
  - When to use: specifying rub-in, play time, pumpability, brush-mark leveling.
  - How: pseudoplastic (xanthan, guar, carbomers, cellulosics, clays, silicas) thins and recovers immediately; thixotropic thins and recovers only with time — control rest time before measuring viscosity.
- **Shear-sensitive vs. shear-loving modifiers**: large polymers and silicas break down under high shear; clays, organoclays and some gums (carrageenan) tolerate or even build viscosity with shear.
  - When to use: scale-up, homogenizer and pump selection.
- **Two routes to emulsion stability**: (1) thicken or add yield value to the external phase (most common) to slow droplet movement; (2) thicken the internal phase so droplets resist deformation, delaying creaming/coalescence.
- **Appearance/texture-driven selection**: clear → synthetic polymer, carbomer, polyethylene; opaque → clays/organoclays; gloss → starch derivatives or gums; short texture → starch derivative (lotion) or silica (toothpaste); long texture → gums, gum derivatives, highly substituted cellulosics.
- **Salt curve**: plot viscosity vs NaCl level in anionic surfactant systems; select the level just before the peak.

## Key Concepts
- **Thixotropic**: shear-thinning with delayed recovery after shear stops; cooling rate and rest time affect structure, making accelerated stability data hard to reproduce if timing is uncontrolled.
- **Pseudoplastic**: shear-thinning with immediate recovery.
- **Yield value**: stress needed to start flow; raising it improves suspension and even distribution of actives/pigments.
- **Syneresis**: water separating from a gel; starch derivatives inhibit it, extending shelf life.
- **Suspension mechanism**: suspending agents form a loose structure that traps solids; if particle weight exceeds network strength it settles — fine particles suspend more easily than large ones.
- **Pituitous (stringy) gums**: can pull back into small pump orifices, causing dispensing problems.
- **Synthetic polymer architecture**: homopolymer (one monomer), copolymer (more than one), crosspolymer (copolymer + cross-linker); mostly acrylic acid based; supplied tightly coiled in acid form, uncoiled by neutralization; thicken by swelling, not dissolving (entanglement + hydrogen bonding).
- **Organoclays**: hydrophilic clays reacted with long-chain quats (e.g., quaternium-18 hectorite); platelet hydrogen-bond structure in oils, esters, silicones.
- **Lipstick suspension**: pigments must stay suspended while molten and not settle into the tip after molding, or batch shade and appearance vary.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Screen candidates against: formula pH, processing temperature, shear history (lab and plant), packaging/dispensing, consumer handling, clarity target.
- For thixotropic systems, fix rest time and cooling rate before viscosity readings and stability reporting.
- Hand lotion → fast rub-in; massage lotion/barrier cream → longer play time (e.g., maltodextrin adds play time without big viscosity gain).
- Surfactant systems (shampoo, body wash) are hard to thicken: carbomer, gums, gum/starch derivatives, aluminum magnesium hydroxide stearate, HPMC, or salt.
- Build a salt curve for anionic surfactant bases; choose concentration just before the peak.
- Neutralize acrylic polymers with base to uncoil; keep them away from high salt, extreme pH, high shear, prolonged UV.
- Add silica late and avoid high shear afterward; heat polyethylene to ≥80 °C; give castor derivatives controlled heat + shear; use organoclay pre-dispersions to avoid activation/high-shear steps.
- Nail polish needs flow to level brush marks; organoclays suspend pigment.
- Foam: surfactants create foam, but gums, cellulosics, starch derivatives, fatty alcohols and polyquaterniums stabilize it.
- Sunscreen: film-forming rheology modifiers help distribute UV actives and may improve water resistance.

## Mental Models
- A thickener is a multi-function ingredient: pick it for the full property set (suspension, film, feel, foam), not viscosity alone.
- Particle settling is a contest between particle weight and network strength — shrink the particle or strengthen the network.
- Process history is part of the formula for thixotropic or shear-sensitive systems.
- Several routes usually reach the target; weigh trade-offs (clarity, salt, shear, naturalness) rather than seeking one "right" thickener.

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying on natural gums without controls**: agricultural variability and microbial load cause batch inconsistency.
- **High shear after adding silica or large polymers**: destroys the hydrogen-bonded network/structure; rheology lost.
- **Adding salt past the peak**: viscosity collapses rapidly.
- **Measuring thixotropic viscosity at uncontrolled times**: non-reproducible data.
- **Stringy gums in small-orifice pumps**: product pulls back into the container.
- **Acrylic polymers in UV-exposed sun products without checks**: viscosity loss over time.
