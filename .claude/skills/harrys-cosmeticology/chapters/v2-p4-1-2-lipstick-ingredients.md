# Part 4.1.2: Ingredients for Creating the Next Greatest Lipstick
**Authors**: Nick Morante (Nick Morante Cosmetic Consultants)

## Core Idea
A lipstick is a molded crystalline solid of waxes, oils/esters and colorants whose texture, slip, tack, shine and wear are set by wax/oil ratios. Most lipsticks are "me too" variations on the same ingredients; differentiation comes from new technology (sugar esters, specialty waxes, silicones, film formers, novel powders and rainforest-sourced oils). The core challenge is balancing comfort, shine and wear, which are inherently contradictory.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Holy Grail of Lipsticks" trade-off triangle**: comfort, shine, long wear (no less than 4–6 h).
  - When to use: setting design targets and diagnosing negatives (tack, drag, dullness).
  - How: shine is a function of melt (melting = wearing off); wear needs polymers/film formers (stiff, uncomfortable) or more powder (drying). Balance rather than maximize; unconventional ingredients ease the trade-off.
- **Structure first, aesthetics second**: build an acceptable crystalline stick structure, then tune shine, feel and longevity.
- **Spreading coefficient ranking**: compare emollients by spreading coefficient; lower = drier feel, less spread; spreadability rises with polarity, molecular weight and branching; silicone oils show ≥35.
- **Oil absorbance balancing**: calculate total powder oil absorbance (standard ASTM method); offset with high-absorbance alumina hydrate or low-absorbance barium sulfate.
- **Thumb-and-finger snap test**: squeeze stick; good crystallinity resists then snaps; a flat mushy crush means too soft. (Instrumental alternative: Chatillon.)

## Key Concepts
- **Market**: >50 brands, ~2000 shade names in the US; lipstick sales up almost 15% in 2011; raw cost of a simple lipstick only 5–10¢.
- **Molding**: metal book molds (need flaming to remove seams/flaws) vs silicone molds (unusual shapes, logos, no flaming; silicone-based ingredients may be too compatible with silicone molds and cause problems).
- **Core waxes**: candelilla and carnauba for strength and shine; beeswax for stick shrinkage and mold release; plus Japan, montan, microcrystalline, ceresin, high-mp polyethylene. Synthetic beeswax and Japan wax are cheaper with same physical properties. Wax grades differ in hardness, melt point, color and odor.
- **Melt point window**: stable crystalline structure should melt between 65°C and 75°C; one global formula must survive equatorial summer and Canadian winter.
- **Hard vs soft waxes**: hard waxes melt over ~3–5°C with sharp transition; soft waxes over ~10–12°C with gradual viscosity change.
- **Drop point**: relative measure of melt viscosity, not the melt point; rheology keeps stick from flowing when warm.
- **Castor oil**: inexpensive polar solvent and good pigment wetter, main grinding solvent, but not mandatory.
- **Sweating**: liquid droplets on stick surface as temperature rises; sweating at room temperature signals ingredient incompatibility; keep nonpolar ingredients minimal; fix with clay master gels.
- **Wear**: average lipstick ~4 h or less; ideal ≥6 h; >6 h is a major challenge.
- **Silicone waxes**: solid-to-viscous-liquid transition at/just above room temperature → creamy laydown; compatible with paraffin; higher MW than paraffins.
- **Alkyl dimethicones**: oil-soluble (mineral oil, petrolatum, esters), insoluble in dimethicone; longer alkyl → harder, higher melt (C32+ can melt ≥60°C); more silicone → softer.
- **Phenyl silicones** (trimethylsiloxyphenyl dimethicone): higher refractive index → more shine; 20–1000 cps; compatible with esters, not hydrocarbons; reduce tack of acrylic polymers.
- **Siliconyl waxes** (beeswax, carnauba, candelilla, polyethylene): improve compatibility of other silicones.
- **Synthetic by-product waxes**: supply risk if the parent process changes; forced reformulation.
- **Omega fatty acids**: omega-3 (linolenic), -6 (linoleic), -9 (oleic, olive oil), -7 (macadamia, sea buckthorn); kukui nut oil rich in omega-3/6; support restorative claims.
- **Staining pigments**: bromofluorescein lakes (D&C Red 22, 28, Orange 5) start as pH-sensitive water-soluble dyes, stain lips pink after wear; hygroscopic, dry lips at high levels.
- **Pigment overload**: shifts crystalline to amorphous, soft/mushy, breaks at the cup line.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Keep stick melt point 65–75°C for global stability.
- Balance polar and nonpolar solvents; most lipstick ingredients are polar; keep nonpolar minimal to avoid sweating.
- Use bentonite/hectorite pre-made master gels to stop sweating and reduce flow at temperature.
- Grind colorants in a suitable solvent (often castor oil) with ball mill or three-roll mill, or buy pre-ground dispersions.
- Rising color load comes out of the oils → drier feel; consider softer formula for payoff instead.
- Mica effect pigments restricted to ≤150 µm in some eye products; 150 µm pearls feel raspy on lips—avoid for sensitive users.
- Avoid volatile fluids unless needed; if used, package must be airtight (adds cost).
- Hydrocarbon/mineral-wax-only sticks fall into concealer/foundation stick categories; hydrocarbons give occlusivity but poor slip/shine.
- Use low levels of film formers/adhesives to extend wear without tackiness; pair with high-slip ingredients.
- Use silicone ingredients cautiously with silicone molds.
- Do not label boron nitride and similar synthetic "mineral" powders as natural.
- Partner with suppliers ("shop around") for new silicone and wax technology.

## Mental Models
- Shine = melt = wear-off: any shine gain must be paid for elsewhere.
- Same INCI ≠ same performance: wax grade/fraction determines hardness and melt.
- Bench experience matters: feel (snap, glide) is learned by making batches.
- Technology, not ingredient permutation, creates the point of difference.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Me too" reformulation**: reshuffling the same waxes/esters yields no distinction.
- **Excessive pigment/pearl loading**: amorphous, mushy sticks that break at the cup line.
- **Too much staining dye**: hygroscopic, dries lips.
- **Nonpolar excess**: incompatibility and sweating.
- **Relying on single-source synthetic by-product waxes**: availability can vanish.
- **Making clay gels in-house**: hard to build viscosity correctly.
