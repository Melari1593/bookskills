# Part 3.3.1: An Overview of the Physical and Chemical Properties of Hair and Their Relation to Cosmetic Needs, Performance and Properties
**Authors**: Manuel Gamez-Garcia, Ph.D. (Ashland Specialty Ingredients)

## Core Idea
Hair is not "dead" material: it is an optimized assembly of proteins, lipids and polysaccharides whose native protein conformation still carries biological activity (like an enzyme, which is lifeless but functional). Its softness, strength, moisture/heat buffering, shape memory and optics come from the architecture of cortical and cuticle cells. Heat, alkali, reduction, bleaching, UV and mechanical fatigue denature these proteins and break bonds, compromising both function and aesthetics; good cosmetics keep proteins moisturized (plasticized) and in their native conformation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Feughelman two-phase model (1959)**: hair as crystalline keratin microfibrils (C phase, rods) in parallel with an amorphous protein matrix (M phase).
  - When to use: explaining moisture-dependent stiffness, softness, and stress-strain behavior.
  - How: C phase = elastic, non-swelling micro-springs; M phase = "gel" (hydrophilic, water-plasticized) + "non-gel" (hydrophobic, highly cross-linked, softens only at high T). Matrix has low-sulfur zone near fibrils and high-sulfur zone further away.
- **Stress-strain three-region curve**: Hookean (0 to 2-5%, depends on RH; H-bonded amorphous + alpha keratin), yield (2-35%; alpha-helix unfolding to beta "ladder/platelet" keratin), post-yield (>35%; recrystallization of amorphous phase, then break).
  - When to use: interpreting tensile tests, grooming damage (combing wet hair reaches yield region).
- **Hair as thermoset viscoelastic shape-memory biopolymer**: ~90% of hair is disulfide cross-linked cortex, so it has a permanent "remembered" shape; temporary styles are held then recovered.
  - How: fast deformations (head movement, finger-through) -> elastic; long-duration (sleeping on bent hair, styling) -> viscous + delayed elastic recovery.
- **Follicle as assembly line**: three zones bottom-to-top: proliferation (dermal papilla + basal membrane, stem cells from bulge divide), gene expression/differentiation (cuticle, cortex, medulla cells), keratinization (apoptosis + disulfide cross-linking, mid-follicle).
- **Follicle cycle**: anagen (active growth, ~4-6 years; properties set here, nutrition/hormone dependent), catagen (2-4 weeks, apoptosis), telogen (2-3 months, old hair shed); substages and overlap between new anagen and old telogen exist.

## Key Concepts
- **Composition**: mostly keratin protein; lipids + polysaccharides 2-10% of fiber weight (mainly in cell cement and surface). Rich in half-cystine (17.8%), serine (11.7%), glutamic acid (11.4%), proline (8.4%).
- **Water as part of hair**: bound water stabilizes keratin secondary structure, persists even at 0% RH, removed only near denaturation temperatures; free/bulk water causes swelling and plasticization.
- **Keratin types**: alpha (soft; hair, nails, skin, wool) vs beta (hard; reptile claws, turtle shells); alpha keratins type I (acidic) and type II (basic), always coiled in pairs in hair.
- **Cortical cells**: spindle-shaped, ~20 µm diameter, ~100 µm long; synthesize keratin into intermediate filaments bound via disulfides into matrix forming macrofibrils; cemented by the cell membrane complex (CMC). Breaking strength comparable to a copper wire of equal diameter; composite like glass-fiber-reinforced epoxy.
- **Thermal transitions**: crystalline content ~95% -> Tm ~156 °C; Tg ~144 °C (amorphous matrix). (Text also says hair softens "above 174 °C" — inconsistent figure in source.)
- **Water plasticization**: most significant at deformations below 2%; 2-40% deformation response controlled by alpha->beta unfolding rather than water. Mechanically induced alpha->beta transition is fully reversible; heat- or chemically induced is usually irreversible. Deformations >5% occur during repetitive combing, especially wet detangling.
- **Water sorption isotherm**: sigmoidal with hysteresis (absorption from dry is lower than desorption from wet). At 100% RH hair absorbs up to ~30% water, mostly in cortex.
- **Heat buffering**: 1 kg dry wool going 40->70% RH releases ~164 kJ; dry wool absorbing ~35% water releases ~960 kJ (~8 h of electric blanket). Most heat released at low RH (multilayer adsorption on amino/carboxyl/H-bond sites).
- **Limited swelling**: hair cells keep integrity in water (cross-linked), unlike stratum corneum corneocytes which swell without limit.
- **Ortho- vs para-cortical cells**: straight hair mostly para; curly hair has both in separate regions; more ortho -> curlier. Ortho absorbs less moisture -> differential plasticization explains curl enhancement when moistened. Meso-cortex exists in other mammals, not humans.
- **Cuticle**: flat cells ~0.5 µm thick, stacked 5-11 deep like roof shingles. Layers outward-in: F-layer (18-MEA covalently bound to ultra-high-sulfur protein; lubricity/hydrophobicity), epicuticle (~120 nm), exocuticle (~250 nm) (highest sulfur in fiber), endocuticle (non-cross-linked, negatively charged, swellable, soft "cushion"), CMC.
- **CMC**: upper beta / delta / lower beta layers. Beta lipids covalently bound on one side, physically (dispersion forces) on the delta side -> weakest mechanical link. Between cuticle cells beta = 18-MEA monolayer; between cortical cells = bilayers of fatty acids, cholesterol, ceramides (physically bound). Delta layer (part hydrophilic/hydrophobic) is the main diffusion route into cortex.
- **Failure sites**: dry -> CMC junction fails (cuticle chipping in dry combing, breaking); wet -> endocuticle fails.
- **Cuticle buckling/lifting**: caused by viscoelastic mismatch between cuticle layers under tensile elongation (combing) or thermal stress (blow-drying, flat irons; rapid compression from water evaporation). Always involves CMC breakage. Rare near root, common at tips (cumulative oxidation weakens cement). Mostly reversible with time; a fraction is permanent. Once buckled, cells re-lift from mere water swelling, though CMC can partially re-cement if gaps close.
- **Cuticle moisture**: buckled cuticles did not recover at 25 °C / 53% RH, but did recover after 1 min water immersion or when moisture condenses (surface above dew point) -> cuticle needs liquid water / multilayer adsorption.
- **Dryness perception**: swatches at high RH scored drier; perceived dryness tracks surface roughness / cuticle condition, not cortex moisture.
- **Optics**: cuticle sheath refractive index ~1.53. Specular reflection = shine; diffuse = matte/dull. Refracted light reaches melanin, giving color. Lifted cuticles with air micro-pockets create light interference patterns (LIPs, iridescence/structural color) — too small and incoherent, so net effect is dulling. Virgin layered cuticle produces weak iridescence contributing to natural color.
- **Wet-hair shine**: water fills air pores (less scattering) and changes endocuticle RI (enhances iridescence); PDMS, oils and some high-MW polymers do the same if they fill cuticle air gaps or smooth the surface.
- **Medulla**: low-density, porous, discontinuous core of large cuboidal cells; mostly in thicker hair, variable even on one head; air-filled, possibly thermal insulation; affects optics.
- **Melanin**: granules ~0.8 µm; polymers of DHI and DHICA; act as radical scavengers, antioxidants, UV/heat/gamma absorbers. Eumelanin (brown, dark) and pheomelanin (red). Gray = absent melanin; blond = low brown eumelanin. Made by melanocytes, transferred into cortical cells by phagocytosis.
- **Follicle numbers**: 100,000-150,000 scalp follicles in clusters of 3-4; number constant through life; one fiber per follicle; regenerates ~every 4-6 years.
- **Pilosebaceous unit**: follicle + sebaceous gland act as peripheral hormone-producing unit (steroids, neuropeptides, neurotrophins); sebum/enzymes help maintain shaft. Disorders (traction, androgenetic, chemical alopecia, trichorrhexis nodosa) result from interference with follicle biochemistry.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Keep the amorphous matrix plasticized (moisturized) and avoid denaturing native proteins: minimize hot irons, perm, bleach, alkaline straightening, prolonged UV.
- Wet combing/detangling pushes fibers into the yield region (>5%, up to 35%); detangling aids reduce this load.
- Cuticle recovery requires liquid water contact (immersion, condensation), not ambient humidity; a brief water rinse can reverse reversible buckling.
- Blow-drying with low-MW oils on wet hair can produce cuticle buckling.
- Shine/color enhancers (silicones such as PDMS, oils, high-MW polymers) work by filling cuticle air gaps or smoothing cuticle to reduce diffuse scattering.
- Wool literature is a valid starting point for hair research (same keratin architecture, hydrophobic exterior/hydrophilic interior).
- Test methods named: stress-strain tensile curves at controlled RH, water sorption isotherms, SEM, optical microscopy with light interference pattern (LIP) technique, DSC-type thermal transitions.
- Product benefits can target the living follicle (anagen-phase nutrition, hormones) as well as the keratinized shaft.

## Mental Models
- Hair = fiber-reinforced composite: crystalline springs (elastic) + water-plasticizable matrix (viscous); moisture tunes the matrix only.
- Time-scale lens: same hair is elastic to fast forces and viscous to slow ones — the basis of styling hold and "bed head."
- Weakest-link thinking: damage localizes at the CMC (dry) or endocuticle (wet); design treatments around those junctions.
- Appearance is a readout of structural integrity: dullness = lifted cuticle scattering; perceived dryness = surface roughness.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating hair as inert dead matter**: ignores that native protein conformation is functional; denaturation degrades moisture control, mechanics and optics.
- **Equating "dry hair" with low cortex moisture**: perception correlates with surface roughness; high-RH swatches can feel drier.
- **Assuming humid air re-moisturizes damaged cuticles**: buckled cuticles need condensed/liquid water.
- **Aggressive thermal styling with oils on wet hair**: rapid evaporative compression buckles cuticle and breaks CMC; damaged cells re-lift on later wetting.
