# Part 3.4: Substrate: The Nails
**Authors**: Dr. Lawrence Silverberg (NailPure)

## Core Idea
The nail unit is a specialized keratin appendage whose appearance is governed by the matrix (which produces the plate), the nailbed (adhesion and color) and the folds (seal against infection). Most unsightly nail conditions trace back to disruption of a specific site of the unit, to infection, or to external chemical/mechanical insults, including nail cosmetics themselves. Formulators need to know which problems are cosmetic and which signal disease that needs referral.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Nail unit anatomy map**: nail plate (corpus unguis), nailbed, medial/lateral nail grooves (folds), distal groove (hyponychium), epionychium, posterior nail groove (cuticle), nail root/matrix (incl. lunula).
  - When to use: locating where a defect starts and what product or habit could cause it.
  - How: dorsal plate is made by proximal matrix, mid-plate by mid-matrix, ventral plate by distal matrix; so plate thickness depends on matrix length, and a defect at a given plate depth points to a specific matrix region.
- **Classification of nail disease**: (a) by site/kind: conditions of the nail itself, onychodystrophies, nail signs of dermatitis, nail signs of systemic disease, congenital; (b) by cause: infection, psoriasis, contact dermatitis, eczematous dermatitis, hypovitaminosis, tumor, trauma, genetic.
  - When to use: triaging consumer complaints and claim scope.
  - How: separate cosmetic complaints (brittleness, splitting, mild ridging) from signs that need medical review (Beau's lines, pigmented bands, onychomycosis, paronychia).

## Key Concepts
- **Nail plate**: hard keratin; unlike skin it does not shed as scales; much lower lipid than skin, so much less water flux across it.
- **Nailbed**: living dermis with capillaries makes the attached plate look pink; the free distal edge looks white.
- **Hyponychium**: seal where skin meets the plate at the fingertip; protects the bed from infection and trauma.
- **Cuticle (posterior nail groove)**: thin epithelium over the proximal plate, formed by the ventral side of the proximal nail fold; losing it opens the way to paronychia.
- **Matrix/root**: germinal tissue extending 5–10 mm proximal to the posterior groove; the lunula is its visible half-moon, probably showing incomplete keratinization.
- **Development**: first signs at week 9 in utero; matrix at week 11; adult keratinization by week 20; hard plate by week 32. Toenails run ~4 weeks behind.
- **Nailbed epidermis**: lacks stratum lucidum and granulosum, hair follicles and sweat glands; rete ridges interlock with the plate in parallel, fingerprint-like rows; the bed moves forward with the plate.
- **Composition**: hard, high-sulfur keratin (cystine ~9–12% of nail weight); fibrils run parallel to the surface, side to side; moisture 7–12%; fat 0.15–0.76% (1.38% in infants); calcium 0.02–0.04% and does **not** contribute to hardness; water content tracks ambient relative humidity (Baden).
- **Growth**: continuous for life, set by matrix turnover. Fingernails 0.5–1.2 mm/week; toenails 0.2–0.4 mm/week. Fastest on the 3rd finger, slowest on the 5th; right hand slightly faster; no sex difference at ages 19–23. Faster in summer, in psoriasis, pregnancy and nail-biting; slower in cold, viral infections (measles, mumps), yellow nail syndrome, starvation. Full replacement: fingernail 5–6 months; toenail 12–18 months.
- **Aging and pregnancy**: aging nails thicken, grow more slowly and get diseases more easily; in pregnancy expect transverse grooves, more brittleness and softening.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Nail pliability depends on water. Products for brittle or splitting nails should restore and hold moisture: rub emollients in after bathing and use them nightly, and avoid frequent immersion in surfactant solutions.
- Onycholysis care: trim off the detached distal plate (usually painless), then put emollient on the bed and distal plate daily so the nail reattaches as it grows out.
- Watch for green (Pseudomonas) or yellow (Candida) color under a lifted nail. This means colonization and is not a cosmetic stain.
- Brittleness and conventional lacquer: in a study of 25 businesswomen using the same lacquer, about half showed brittleness/splitting and the rest did not, so the book says lacquer is not supported as the cause. Frequent polish and remover use is still listed as a likely cause of onychoschizia (horizontal splitting).
- Aggressive cuticle trimming or pulling removes the barrier and leads to paronychia. Cuticle products and instructions should protect the cuticle rather than remove it.
- Any new or rapidly changing pigmented band must be referred to a dermatologist for possible biopsy, because it could be melanoma.
- Plan claims timing around growth rate. A fingernail takes 5–6 months to replace (toenail 12–18 months), so visible plate improvement needs months.

## Mental Models
- **Site → defect mapping**: matrix damage gives plate defects (pits, Beau's lines, ridges); nailbed changes give color or detachment; fold damage gives paronychia. Find the location first, then the cause.
- **Water, not calcium, governs nail hardness and flexibility**: calcium is only 0.02–0.04% and does not harden nails, and plate water follows humidity.
- **Nail as a record of time**: the plate carries a timeline of past systemic events (Beau's lines) at a distance set by growth rate.
- **Cosmetics can be the cause**: hardeners, acrylics, UV dryers and tints all appear among the causes of nail disorders.

## Anti-patterns
- **Blaming conventional nail lacquer for brittleness**: the book says there is no support for this.
- **Formaldehyde hardeners / phenol varnish / photobonded acrylics**: implicated in onycholysis and contact dermatitis.
- **Aggressive cuticle cutting/pulling**: breaks the seal and causes acute or chronic paronychia.
- **Treating every white nail as leukonychia**: whitening can come from the nailbed, not the plate.
- **Treating pigmented bands cosmetically**: misses melanoma; refer instead.
- **Constant wet work**: frequent hand-washing or immersion drives chronic paronychia, brittleness and splitting.
