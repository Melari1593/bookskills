# Part 2.2: An Overview of the Changing Regulatory Landscape in the U.S. and the E.U. and How to Deal with Them
**Authors**: Dr. Matteo Zanotti Russo (University of Pisa; Angel Consulting SAS, Milano)

## Core Idea
Both the US (since 1938) and the EU (Directive 76/768, now Regulation 1223/2009) rest on the same principle: the company placing the product on the market is responsible for its safety and labeling, with post-market surveillance instead of pre-market approval (Japan adopted this in its 2001 deregulation). The EU differs mainly in formal documentation duties (PIF, CPSR, CPNP notification, mandatory GMP), while toxicological assessment criteria are shared. A product can be sold in both markets only if "simultaneous compliance" is planned from the start; meeting the EU standard makes US compliance easier.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **US adulterated vs misbranded tests (FD&C Act Sec. 601/602)**: the two ways a cosmetic is illegal.
  - When to use: compliance check of any US product.
  - How: adulterated = contains poisonous/deleterious substance injurious under labeled or customary use (hair-dye exception), filthy/putrid/decomposed matter, made/held under insanitary conditions, harmful container, or unsafe color additive. Misbranded = false/misleading labeling, missing required info, info not prominent, misleading container fill/form, non-conforming color additive, or violation of Poison Prevention Packaging Act 1970.
- **Intended-use classification (US cosmetic vs drug)**: classification follows intended use, not only composition.
  - When to use: before writing claims.
  - How: intended use is inferred from labeling/advertising/internet, consumer perception (reputation), and ingredients with known action (fluoride, UV filters). Drug-triggering claims: hair regrowth, cellulite reduction, varicose vein treatment, skin whitening (melanin reduction) or darkening, skin cell regeneration.
- **US safety substantiation**: (a) rely on toxicological data on ingredients and similar products, plus (b) additional testing as appropriate; FDA recommends finished-product toxicology tests.
- **EU multi-level ingredient approach**: positive lists (Annex IV colorants, V preservatives, VI UV filters), negative lists (Annex II prohibited, Annex III restricted), all other ingredients free but must be safe.
- **SCCS 4-phase risk assessment (Notes of Guidance, 8th ed.)**: 1 hazard identification, 2 dose–response (NOAEL), 3 exposure assessment, 4 risk characterization; steps 3–4 compulsory under 1223/2009.
  - How: MoS = NOAEL / SED (systemic exposure dosage); MoS must be above 100.
- **EU compliance roadmap for a US firm (4 steps)**: 1 appoint RP and Safety Assessor; 2 under SA, verify GMP–ISO 22716; 3 set up PIFs and labels; 4 notify in CPNP.
- **REACH decision path**: per ingredient, check EU tonnage → exemptions → registration or substitution (see Practical Guidance).

## Key Concepts
- **US cosmetic definition (FD&C Act Sec. 201(i))**: articles rubbed, poured, sprinkled, sprayed on or otherwise applied to the body for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness or altering appearance, and their components; excludes soap. Cosmetic acts physically, not physiologically.
- **Cosmetic/drug (US)**: product with cosmetic function that also treats/prevents disease or affects structure/function; must meet both regimes. Examples: anti-dandruff shampoos, antiperspirant-deodorants, fluoride toothpaste, moisturizers and makeup with SPF.
- **"Cosmeceutical"**: not recognized by FDA; a physiologically active cosmetic is a drug or cosmetic/drug.
- **Cosmetic/drug obligations**: NDA (sec. 505) or OTC monograph compliance; drug GMP 21 CFR 210/211; drug registration via DRLS/eDRLS (21 CFR 207); "Drug Facts" label (21 CFR 201.66) with Active Ingredients then Inactive Ingredients.
- **Soap (21 CFR 701.20)**: bulk of nonvolatile matter is alkali salts of fatty acids, detergency due to them, and labeled/sold solely as soap; regulated by CPSC, not FDA. Adding cosmetic claims (fragrance, moisturizing, deodorant) or using synthetic detergents makes it a cosmetic.
- **VCRP**: US Voluntary Cosmetic Registration Program; registration not mandatory, no premarket approval.
- **Color additives (US)**: positive list, 21 CFR Parts 73, 74, 81, 82; "exempt from certification" (mineral/plant/animal) vs "subject to batch certification" (coal-tar/petroleum-derived; each batch analyzed and FDA-approved).
- **EU cosmetic definition (Art. 2)**: substance/mixture for external parts (epidermis, hair, nails, lips, external genitals), teeth and oral mucosa, exclusively or mainly to clean, perfume, change appearance, protect, keep in good condition or correct body odours. Two cumulative criteria: application site + primary function.
- **No cosmetic/drug combo in EU**: pharmacological function not allowed; sunscreens are cosmetics in the EU (filters "protect").
- **Responsible Person (RP, Art. 4–5)**: EU-established natural/legal person accepting the role in writing; responsible for Art. 3 safety, 8 GMP, 10 safety assessment, 11 PIF, 12 analysis, 13 notification, 14–16 restricted/CMR/nano, 17 traces, 18 animal testing, 19 labeling, 20 claims, 21 public info, 23 serious undesirable effects, 24 substance info.
- **Safety Assessor (Art. 10)**: "double role": writes the safety assessment and scientifically supervises most RP obligations; must hold an EU-recognized scientific degree; CV must show years of cosmetic experience, manufacturing and packaging knowledge, raw material impurity knowledge, toxicology and microbiology.
- **Safety (Art. 3)**: safe under normal or reasonably foreseeable use, considering presentation, labeling, instructions for use/disposal and any RP information.
- **PIF (Art. 11)**: product description; CPSR; manufacturing method + GMP statement; proof of claimed effects where justified; data on any animal testing (including for third-country requirements). Kept at the label address, available to authorities on request (usually after significant complaints).
- **CPSR (Annex I)**: Part A (safety information) 1 composition; 2 physico-chemical properties & stability; 3 microbiological quality + challenge test; 4 impurities, traces, packaging material; 5 normal/foreseeable use; 6 product exposure; 7 substance exposure; 8 toxicological profiles (all routes, systemic effects, MoS on NOAEL, or justify absence); 9 undesirable/serious undesirable effects; 10 product information. Part B (assessment) 1 conclusion; 2 label warnings/instructions; 3 reasoning (MoS, specific assessment for children under 3 and external intimate hygiene, interactions, stability impacts); 4 assessor credentials.
- **Article 17 traces**: Annex II substances tolerated only if from natural/synthetic ingredients, process or packaging migration, technically unavoidable under GMP, and safe; no legal limits, so the Safety Assessor sets them (RAPEX cases show applied limits).
- **CMR (Reg. 1272/2008)**: Cat. 2 prohibited unless SCCS-approved; Cat. 1A/1B prohibited unless food-compliant (Reg. 178/2002), no suitable alternatives, specific use with known exposure, and SCCS found safe.
- **Animal testing (Art. 18)**: banned for products and ingredients; OECD-validated alternatives accepted; repeated-dose, reproductive toxicity and toxicokinetics with special approval. Industry strategies: read-across from similar ingredients, QSAR, prior-art data from pharma uses.
- **CPNP notification (Art. 13)**: category and name; RP name/address where PIF kept; origin country for imports; Member State of marketing; physical contact person; nanomaterials; CAS/EC numbers of CMR 1A/1B; frame formulation for medical treatment; plus original label and packaging photo. Not an authorization. Distributors notify only when translating label elements.
- **Market surveillance (Art. 22–23)**: Member States monitor and review every 4 years; RP must report serious undesirable effects promptly with product ID and corrective measures.
- **RAPEX**: EU rapid alert system for non-food, non-drug consumer products; weekly public reports with name, image, cause, action; subscribing is a cheap early-warning tool.
- **REACH (cited as Reg. 1906/2007)**: exemptions <1 t/yr, natural substances (as such or extracted with water only), polymers (>50% repeating units).
- **INCI in the US**: Cosmetic Ingredient Dictionary by PCPC (ex-CTFA); biennial until 2010, web-based wINCI from 2011. New names requested online (structure, impurities/solvents, process description, proposed name). INCI listing is not an authorization; absence does not mean prohibited.
- **INCI in the EU**: Glossary (Art. 33) awaited; meanwhile Decision 2006/257/EC inventory (official) or CosIng (updated, non-official). On Glossary publication, old names must be dropped within 12 months. EU name assignment is managed by the Commission and slower than PCPC.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- First safety step (US and EU): screen formula against prohibited/restricted lists; then assess unregulated ingredients and impurities, then test finished product.
- US: without expert safety substantiation, label must read "Warning—The safety of this product has not been determined" (21 CFR 740.10).
- Even in the US, review raw-material impurities (FDA campaigns found lead in makeup); use EU annexes as reference if exporting.
- Typical finished-product tests (industry benchmark): human patch test or in vitro skin; mucous membrane irritation in vitro/HET-CAM; preservative challenge test.
- Micro limits commonly applied (not official in US): total aerobic count 1000 cfu/g; 500 cfu/g for eye-area and other critical products. Sterility is pointless; rely on preservative systems and hygienic raw materials (GMP).
- US labeling: PDP carries name/identity, §740.10 warning, net contents (in bottom 30% of PDP); information panels carry directions, warnings, name/place of business, ingredients. No required info on the bottom unless the item is small and picked up by hand.
- US letter sizes: ingredients ≥1/16 in (≥1/32 in if label area <12 sq in); warnings ≥1/16 in; letter height measured on lower-case "o".
- US ingredient order: descending predominance; ≤1% ingredients any order after >1%; color additives any order at end; drug actives first ("Active Ingredient"); FDA-accepted trade secrets as "and other ingredients" (§720.8); flavor/fragrance excluded from individual listing.
- US mandatory warnings: aerosols (avoid eyes, pressurized, do not puncture/incinerate, do not store above 120°F, keep from children; inhalation-misuse warning); feminine deodorant sprays (spray ≥8 inches from skin); foaming bath products (irritation of skin and urinary tract; adult-only variant).
- EU label: RP name/address; nominal content (except <5 g/5 ml, samples, single-use); date of minimum durability if durability ≤30 months, otherwise PAO (open-jar symbol); precautions (Annex III–VI at least); batch number; function unless obvious; ingredient list (packaging alone allowed), descending order, <1% any order, "[nano]" after nano ingredients, colorants at end, "may contain"/"+/-" for shade ranges.
- EU nominal-content figure height (Dir. 76/211): ≤50 g/ml 2 mm; 50–200 g/ml 3 mm; 200 g/ml–1 kg/l 4 mm; >1 kg/l 6 mm.
- EU small products: if ingredients/warnings cannot fit, use leaflet/tag/card with hand-on-book symbol; soaps/bath balls may show ingredients on a notice near the display container.
- GMP: EU requires ISO 22716:2007 (relevant harmonized standard), certification not required but compliance must be stated in the PIF; ISO 9001-certified sites adapt easily. The FDA Plant Inspection Checklist (buildings, equipment, personnel, raw materials, production, lab controls, records, labeling, complaints, other) maps onto ISO 22716.
- REACH steps: 1 compute EU tonnage per ingredient (including via cosmetics); 2 if >1 t/yr, check polymer or natural exemption; 3 otherwise REACH applies; 4 ask supplier for its ECHA registration number; 5 if none, register (via appointed representative) or replace ingredient.
- Aerosol cosmetics in the EU must also meet Directive 75/324/EEC.
- EU colorants that are also food colorants must meet food purity criteria (Dir. 95/45/EC); EU colorants need no batch certification; identified by 5-digit Color Index.
- Information sources for safety profiles: FDA "frequently asked" ingredient pages (AHAs/BHAs, color additives, DEA, mercury, nano, parabens, phthalates, talc), CIR monographs, PubMed/TOXNET, SCCS opinions; in the EU, suppliers are the first source.
- Guidance docs: Cosmetics Europe Guidelines on Labeling (2011) and on PIF (2011); EC borderline Guidance (cosmetics vs medicines).

## Mental Models
- The CPSR is written for a hypothetical health authority investigating an adverse event; reason and document accordingly.
- Same science, different paperwork: US and EU share risk-assessment science; the EU demands formal proof before release.
- Intended use (claims + perception + known actives) decides classification, not the chemistry alone.
- The RP is effectively the EU branch of the brand owner, not a mailbox.

## Anti-patterns
- **Choosing any EU-resident as RP**: the role needs decision power and access to confidential manufacturing, purchasing and sales data; an external agency is fast but requires sharing secrets and puts its name on every label, making changes costly.
- **Treating an INCI listing as approval**: INCI presence licenses nothing; only colorants (US) and EU Annex IV–VI substances are positive-listed.
- **Assuming Annex II traces have legal limits**: none are set; the Safety Assessor must justify acceptability.
- **Using drug-type claims on cosmetics**: whitening, regeneration, hair regrowth, cellulite claims convert the product into a drug (US) or out of cosmetics (EU).
- **Relying on NOAEL availability**: NOAELs are rarely published; the assessor must find alternative scientific evidence.
- **Spreading unverified product information online**: rapid sharing can damage the market before authorities validate anything.
