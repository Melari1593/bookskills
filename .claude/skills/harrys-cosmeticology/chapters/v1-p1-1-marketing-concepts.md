# Part 1.1: Marketing Concepts to Empower Technical People
**Authors**: Darrin C. Duber-Smith, MS, MBA (Metropolitan State University Denver)

## Core Idea
Marketing is not advertising or selling; it is the planned execution of a strategy whose primary function is revenue. Under the "Marketing Concept," no product should be developed unless a large enough group of people can be shown to buy it, so product development is driven mostly by market analysis rather than by R&D novelty. Because roughly 80-90% of new products fail, chemists and marketers must cooperate across a structured process (situation analysis, SWOT, Four P's, testing, evaluation) and back every claim with an efficacious formula.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Marketing Concept**: develop no new product unless a sufficiently large segment can be proven to buy it.
  - When to use: before committing R&D resources to any new formula or ingredient story.
  - How: verify need through proof-of-concept testing (interviews, surveys, focus groups); treat the want-vs-need debate as irrelevant.
- **Situation analysis (business analysis) -> SWOT**: objective review of internal (controllable) and external (uncontrollable) factors, summarized as Strengths/Weaknesses (internal) and Opportunities/Threats (external).
  - When to use: after concept testing, before writing the marketing program.
  - How: keep the analysis opinion-free; involve all functions (finance, IT, marketing, R&D, operations) for quality and buy-in; start from the mission statement.
- **Green Marketing Model for Strategic Marketing Planning** (Table 1): Internal analysis -> External analysis -> SWOT -> Objectives & Budget -> Marketing Strategy & Program -> Control & Evaluation.
  - When to use: as the outline of a written strategic marketing plan.
- **Four P's / Marketing Mix**: Product, Price, Place, Promotion; only the "original four" are needed.
  - When to use: once SWOT is done; tailor the mix to a defined segment.
  - How: decide product (ingredients, positioning, packaging, branding) before price; price covers variable + fixed costs and reflects competitor/substitute prices, willingness to pay and channel margins; place depends on product and price; promotion mixes push and pull.
- **Green Marketing New Product-Development Process** (Table 2): 7 steps (see Reference Tables).
  - When to use: as the stage-gate sequence from idea to post-launch review.
- **Three forms of innovation** (classified by the degree of consumer behavior change, not by the ingredient):
  - **Continuous**: almost no behavior change (e.g., hand lotion with a new nonactive ingredient; each new iPhone).
  - **Dynamically continuous**: major change in a minor behavior or minor change in a major behavior (e.g., lotion moving from jar to pump; tablet vs laptop).
  - **Discontinuous**: disruptive, new market application (e.g., hair regrowth; ingestible skin/hair supplements, called perhaps the last true discontinuous innovation in the sector).

## Key Concepts
- **Primary function of marketing**: revenue; marketing must first discover what consumers want/need, including needs they are not yet aware of.
- **New-product failure rate**: about 90% fail to meet expectations and are dropped within a few years; elsewhere stated as 80-90%.
- **Idea sources**: customers, competitors, employees, supply-chain members, universities, R&D, and unrelated industries (e.g., carpet-fiber knowledge applied to hair care; "fibers are fibers"). Open Innovation is the ideation model referenced.
- **Proof of concept**: early screening of ideas via interviews, surveys, focus groups before deeper analysis.
- **Market segmentation**: splitting a market into groups sharing characteristics, combining demographics (age, gender, income), psychographics (behaviors, attitudes from surveys/focus groups) and geographics.
- **Channel margins**: distributors often take 10%+ of retail price; retailers up to 50%; what remains after cost and channel is profit.
- **Distribution intensity**: exclusive, selective, or intensive; channel choice (natural stores vs Walmart/grocery) follows product and price strategy.
- **Push vs pull promotion**: push encourages distributors/retailers to stock; pull informs consumers what the product does (claims) and where to buy it.
- **Promotion mix**: advertising, PR, direct marketing, sales promotion, personal selling; most spend is still traditional media, though digital improves measurement.
- **"Dusting"**: putting a story ingredient in at a non-efficacious level, making the product ineffective while claims are still made.
- **Structure-function claims**: FDA mandate to eliminate false or misleading ones; increasing pressure for products that really work.
- **Patent criteria (U.S.)**: novel, inventive step not obvious to a skilled person, industrial application; exclusive for 20 years. Most cosmetic patents (process, composition of matter) require no behavior change and so are not innovations in the author's sense.
- **Word of mouth**: a result of communication efforts, not a marketing activity.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Make product strategy a joint marketing-R&D decision: e.g., marketing may want certified organic/natural ingredients, but chemists and manufacturing know the constraints of making an excellent product with them.
- Decide ingredients, positioning (differentiated from competitors), packaging and branding before setting price.
- Use an efficacious level of any featured ingredient; avoid dusting.
- Test the finished product for claim substantiation even when actives have been clinically tested.
- Before launch: shelf-aging stability, bacterial contamination and other tests until the formula is robust under foreseeable conditions.
- After formula freeze: finalize labels, containers, applicators; packaging is a major part of the consumer experience.
- Run internal mock-up trials with employees, suppliers, friends and family.
- Consider test marketing in specific stores/regions to reduce risk of failure, accepting that it can tip off competitors (rare in personal care).
- Close every launch with a time-bound evaluation: did it work, why, what to do differently.
- Talk about ingredients in terms of consumer-relevant market applications, not hype about "innovation."

## Mental Models
- **Need first, technology second**: R&D innovations are valuable when they can be fitted into a proven consumer need set and are feasible for manufacturing.
- **Innovation = behavior change**: judge novelty by how much consumer behavior changes, not by how new the molecule is; a truly groundbreaking ingredient/application can be introduced only once.
- **Overpromise is expensive**: customers are costly to acquire, and unhappy ones broadcast failures online; claims must match what the formula delivers.
- **Flying blind**: launching without business analysis, market testing and research is like flying without radar/GPS.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Marketing sells whatever R&D develops"**: non-need-based products seldom succeed in hypercompetitive markets.
- **Marketing and R&D working in silos**: the wrong product gets made for the wrong target market; a frequent cause of failure.
- **Calling every new ingredient "innovative"**: desensitizes and numbs the target market to the message; "hot" ingredients are usually just popular.
- **Dusting**: ineffective products, regulatory exposure and brand damage.
- **Telling consumers what they want to hear**: misleading claims lose customers and draw scrutiny from media, NGOs, government and competitors.
- **Relying on universities for training**: theory is not application; companies should hire for aptitude and train intensively.
- **Pricing only on cost**: ignores demand-side (competitor, substitute, willingness to pay) and channel margins.

**Three fixes for product success ("missing links")**: (1) more cross-functional cooperation (marketing, R&D, manufacturing); (2) more dual-degree staff (chemistry + MBA/MS-Marketing, 1-2 extra years); (3) more in-company training.
