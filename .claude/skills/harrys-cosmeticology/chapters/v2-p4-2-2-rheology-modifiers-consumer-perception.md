# Part 4.2.2: Rheology Modifiers and Consumer Perception
**Authors**: Lisa Gandolfi, Ph.D. (Clariant Corporation); Ramiro Galleguillos, Ph.D. (Lubrizol Advanced Materials)

## Core Idea
Every formula has a rheological character — viscosity, yield stress and viscoelasticity over shear rate and time — that governs stability, look in the pack, application and skin feel. Synthetic polymeric rheology modifiers (AMPS-based, polyacrylic acid/carbomer, ASE/HASE) let formulators engineer that character through co-monomer choice, cross-link density, molecular weight and hydrophobic modification. Measured rheology (especially in the presence of skin-level NaCl) correlates with consumer sensory descriptors such as cushion, break, wetness, spreadability and afterfeel.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three rheological parameters**: viscosity (resistance to flow vs shear), yield stress (stability/suspension), viscoelasticity (look at rest, pick-up, cushion).
  - When to use: specifying any thickened product.
  - How: viscosity curves (η vs shear rate, or stress vs rate); yield stress by extrapolating stress to zero shear rate; oscillatory G′, G″, tan δ.
- **Hydrodynamic-volume thickening**: polymer in a good solvent expands → larger hydrodynamic volume → higher viscosity; poor solvent → collapse → lower viscosity.
- **Rheology–sensory map for emulsions**: four sensory properties tied to rheology — speed of breakdown, spreadability, cushion, afterfeel.
  - How: link salt sensitivity → breakdown/wetness; shear thinning → spreadability; G′ (elasticity) → cushion/stiffness; hydrophobic co-monomers → substantive afterfeel.
- **Critical overlap concentration (c*)**: below c* polymer coils don't interact; above, overlap/entanglement gives exponential viscosity rise. Plot viscosity vs concentration in the real solvent system to set use level and judge manufacturing sensitivity.
- **Critical polymer concentration for suspension (cₜ)**: minimum polymer giving enough yield stress to suspend particles, separate from the level needed for target viscosity.
- **Neutralizer-by-alcohol-level selection** for carbomer hydroalcoholic gels (Table below).
- **QDA sensory panel**: 1–6 scales for cushion, quickness of break, wetness, spreadability, afterfeel intensity.

## Key Concepts
- **Newtonian vs pseudoplastic**: stress/rate ratio constant (e.g., polydimethylsiloxane) vs decreasing with shear; these are the two main types in personal care at room temperature.
- **Brookfield viscometers**: relative, instrument-dependent values (spindle, no defined shear rate); cheap and simple. Oscillatory rheometers impose very small strain and give G′, G″, tan δ.
- **Temperature control**: aqueous viscosity can change significantly with only ±1 °C.
- **tan δ = G″/G′**: <1 elastic dominant (stability of shape, stiff, more cushion, poorer pick-up); >1 viscous dominant (flowable, light/fresh, low cushion).
- **Cross-linking**: more cross-linking generally → higher yield stress and viscosity, but molecular weight also matters (CBP934 is most cross-linked yet low MW, so lower yield stress).
- **AMPS polymers**: acryloyldimethyltaurate-based, always cross-linked; permanent negative sulfonate charge independent of pH. Higher charge density/polarity → faster swelling and higher viscosity; higher MW → more thickening at lower dose.
- **Polyacrylic acid (carbomer, acrylates/C10-30 alkyl acrylate crosspolymer)**: pH-dependent; below ~pH 3.5 protonated and collapsed (low viscosity); viscosity rises at pH 3.5–5.0, plateaus at pH 5.5–10, falls above pH 10.
- **ASE/HASE**: methacrylic acid + (meth)acrylate ester latex polymers supplied as low-viscosity liquids; neutralization swells droplets. HASE adds 0–3 mol% hydrophobes (cetyl/stearyl/behenyl) on a PEO spacer → associative bridges and hyperbolic (non-linear) viscosity gain vs ASE's linear gain.
- **Breakdown on skin**: skin NaCl screens anionic charges and osmotically pulls water out of swollen networks → collapse perceived as "break."
- **Crème gels**: emulsions with no traditional emulsifiers; the polymer stabilizes droplets and gives all yield stress, viscoelasticity and shear-thinning. Oils raise yield stress vs aqueous gels.
- **Optical-effects cleansers**: need low yield stress for suspension yet pourable low viscosity; viscosity alone (e.g., salt thickening) does not suspend.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Always control temperature in viscosity measurement; specify instrument, spindle, rpm, temperature (e.g., Brookfield RVT, 20 rpm, 25 °C).
- Validate any yield-stress method with real shelf-life tests; BYV and yield value can mislead (high BYV with poor suspension in SF1/NL10 blends).
- Brookfield Yield Value: BYV = (η@0.5 rpm − η@1.0 rpm)/100.
- Suspension test: ~1% particle cocktail in facial wash (8,200 cP, pH 5.4), 45 °C for one week, score 4 (suspended) to 1 (not suspended).
- Test emulsions with and without 0.05 wt% NaCl to predict on-skin behavior; e.g., Carbopol 940 emulsion G′ drops ~600 → ~150 Pa, Ultrez 20 is nearly unaffected.
- Benchmark cream viscosity window used: 28,000–33,000 mPa·s (RVT, 20 rpm, 25 °C).
- Neutralize carbomers above pH 3.5 (plateau 5.5–10); avoid >pH 10.
- In hydroalcoholic gels with AMPS polymers, propylene glycol barely affects viscosity up to 70:30 PG:water; ethanol lowers it from 20:80, sharply above 60%; acetone hurts most (≈15,000 → 2,000 mPa·s between 60% and 70%).
- Build viscosity–concentration curves in the full formula; emollients and silicones make the rise above c* more gradual.
- For cleansers that must pour and look uniform, use a polymer that provides yield stress at low viscosity rather than salt or PEG-150 distearate.

## Mental Models
- Viscosity says how thick; yield stress says whether it stays stable; viscoelasticity says how it looks and feels — design all three.
- Skin is a salt bath: salt sensitivity becomes a design knob for fast break and light/wet feel vs cushion and richness.
- Use level for stability (cₜ, yield onset) and use level for aesthetics (target viscosity) are different numbers — formulate to the higher one.
- Associative thickening can give huge viscosity without proportional suspending power.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating Brookfield numbers as absolute**: they're instrument-relative with no shear-rate information.
- **Assuming viscosity equals suspension**: salt-thickened shower gel creamed despite matching viscosity.
- **Trusting a yield-stress number without shelf-life validation**: NL10-rich blends had higher yield value yet failed to suspend.
- **Wrong neutralizer for alcohol level**: carbomer salt precipitates out of water/ethanol or water/IPA.
- **Supplier data in pure water applied directly to full formula**: oils, emulsifiers, electrolytes and preservatives change rheology.
