# Part 3.2.1: Classification Scale for Skin Complexions Around the World
**Authors**: Eva Patel (Skin Rx Inc.)

## Core Idea
The Fitzpatrick scale (1975), based only on color and sun response, no longer predicts skin behavior or treatment precautions in a world where the majority of skin is no longer Caucasian. Race, country of origin and ethnicity determine how skin responds to products and procedures, and skin from similar regions behaves similarly. The chapter proposes the 11-category EP Global Skin Classification Scale combining race, country of origin, complexion, skin characteristics, skin type/conditions and precautions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fitzpatrick Classification Scale (1975, Thomas Fitzpatrick, Harvard)**: six types by complexion and sun tolerance.
  - When to use: quick baseline for sunburn/tanning response and skin-cancer risk; customary practice.
  - How: classify I (always burns) to VI (never burns).
  - Limitation: fails to predict skin type and precautions for race, complexion and characteristics.
- **EP Global Skin Classification Scale (© 2012 Eva Patel)**: 11 categories (EP I–XI) with columns Race, Country of Origin, Complexion, Skin Characteristics, Skin Type (typical conditions), Precautions.
  - When to use: choosing peels, lasers, retinoids, hydroquinone and product intensity for diverse clients; anticipating PIH, keloids, melasma.
  - How: identify ancestry (parents/grandparents) and country of origin → map to EP category → apply listed precautions; for mixed ancestry use predominant traits and start mild.
- **Three-page Skin Evaluation questionnaire** (data basis): skin type (8 options from very dry to blemished), ancestry, tanning response, pigmentation tendency, vascularity, acne history, sun exposure, free-radical exposure (smoking, alcohol, diet, exercise, vitamins), lifestyle (sleep, stress, meditation), healing ability (fragility, bruising, scarring, keloid, prior peels/lasers).
  - When to use: initial consultation intake.
  - How: combine with Wood's lamp exam (photodamage, congestion, hyperpigmentation/melasma) and UV photo via Canfield polarized camera; classify primary and secondary conditions to treat.

## Key Concepts
- **Global population context**: Asia 4.2 billion (>60%; China + India ≈37%); Africa ~1 billion (15%); Europe 733 million (11%); Latin America/Caribbean ~600 million (9%); Northern America ~352 million (5%); Oceania ~35 million (0.5%).
- **World population by race**: East Asian 24%, South Asian 21%, Southeast Asian 9%, Black 15%, White 15%, Hispanic 8%, Middle Eastern 8%.
- **US Census 2010 vs 2000**: White 63.7% (from 75.1%), Black 12.2%, Asian 4.7% (from 3.6%), Hispanic/Latino 16.3% (from 12.5%), two or more races 1.9%; total 308,745,538.
- **Study findings (Skin Rejuvenation Clinic, San Francisco, 1997–2005; 588 US residents, mostly female; reviewed 2005–2011)**:
  - Similar geographic/ancestral origin → similar skin types, characteristics and common conditions.
  - Undertones track region: lighter in northern cool climates, darker near the equator; Asian undertones yellow/olive, European undertones ruddy red/pink.
  - Migration does not change original skin type.
  - Many mixed-race clients show combined skin types.
- **Treatment protocol in study**: minimum 8 visits over 4 months (2/month), then monthly maintenance.
- **Questionnaire note**: most sun damage accumulates in the first 18 years of life; residence during first 20 years recorded.
- **Ancestral skin-characteristic profiles**: Anglo-Saxon (fair, dry, thin, early aging, burns, obvious bruising, higher cancer); Southern Mediterranean (oily olive-dark, later aging, cartilage droops, darker thicker scars, cancer rare); Northern European/Scandinavian (fair, thin, early aging, higher cancer); Southern European (dark oily, later aging, less fine wrinkling, bruising lasts longer, thicker darker scars); Irish/Northern England (ruddy freckled, red hair, thin scars, bruises easily, pigmentation problems, skin cancer most common); African (aging very late, little fine wrinkling, keloids possible, thick cartilage, cancer very rare); Asian (aging late, fine wrinkling unusual, pigmentation changes, eyelid surgery harder, cancer very rare).

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Diagnose and treat according to both color and race; one-size-fits-all fails.
- EP I–II: avoid excessive sun and harsh/abrasive treatments; peels/lasers only with caution (EP II).
- EP III–IX: expect hyperpigmentation/PIH tendencies; use caution with deep peels and lasers.
- EP VI–VII: may react to/sensitive to retinoids and hydroquinone — use caution; EP VII, VIII also keloid tendency.
- EP X: extremely sensitive; will hyperpigment and keloid with certain peels/lasers; may react to strong topicals — extreme caution.
- EP XI (mixed): start with milder programs and graduate to stronger products only in absence of irritation; evaluate ancestry before any peel, laser, hydroquinone or retinoid.
- Document daily sunscreen use and SPF (15, 30, 30+) at intake.
- Record age when regular sun-block use began, weekly sun hours, summer/winter sun share, past/current sunbathing, tanning-bed use, personal and family skin-cancer history.
- Record healing ability (fragile/thin skin, easy bruising, scarring, keloids, prior deep peels and lasers) — it predicts procedure risk.
- Use the Wood's lamp at first consultation to reveal photodamage, congestion and hyperpigmentation/melasma not obvious in normal light.
- Use polarized UV photography (Canfield camera) to document UV photodamage as a baseline.
- Categorize each client by primary and secondary skin condition to treat before choosing a program.
- Skin-type section of the scale integrates skin type with sun exposure, environment and lifestyle data.
- Questionnaire skin-type options: Very dry; Dry/mature; Normal to dry (normal T-zone, dry sides); Normal (slightly oily T-zone); Normal to oily; Oily; Very oily (breakouts); Blemished (active acne/acne-prone).

## Mental Models
- **Ancestry over appearance**: country of origin predicts skin behavior better than visible color alone.
- **Skin type is portable**: a client's original skin type travels with them regardless of current residence.
- **Darker/olive = pigment risk; fairer = cancer/early aging risk**: the trade-off shifts along the scale.
- **Mixed ancestry = test-and-escalate**.

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying solely on Fitzpatrick for treatment planning**: ignores race, complexion and characteristics; misses PIH/keloid risk.
- **Aggressive peels/lasers on EP V–X skin without caution**: triggers hyperpigmentation, PIH and keloids.
- **Assuming migration changed skin type**: data show it does not.
