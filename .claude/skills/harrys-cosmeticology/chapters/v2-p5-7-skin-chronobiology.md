# Part 5.7: Chronobiology of the Skin — Skin Circadian Rhythm and Clock Genes: A New Approach to Slowing Down the Aging Process
**Authors**: Nadine Pernodet, Ph.D. and Edward Pelle, Ph.D. (Estée Lauder Research Laboratories)

## Core Idea
Nearly every skin cell carries clock genes synchronized by the master clock in the hypothalamic suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN); 10–20% of genes oscillate on a ~24-hour cycle, timing DNA repair, proliferation, cell cycle, apoptosis and metabolism. Skin spends the day protecting itself and the night repairing, recharging and regenerating. Stress, UV, shift work and aging desynchronize these clocks, causing genome instability, premature aging and cancer risk; keeping skin clocks in sync — and timing products to the skin's rhythm (e.g., night repair, higher night penetration) — is a new route to slowing aging.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Day = protect / Night = repair model**: daytime skin is thickest, pH higher, sebum highest, proliferation low (DNA shielded from UV); night has higher temperature, blood flow, barrier permeability, TEWL, itch, DNA repair and proliferation.
  - When to use: designing day vs. night products and claims.
  - How: day — protection (antioxidants, UV, barrier/sebum support); night — repair/regeneration actives, exploiting higher penetration.
- **Molecular clock loop**: CLOCK/BMAL1 heterodimer binds E-box promoters → transcribes PER1–3 and CRY1–2 → PER/CRY complex re-enters nucleus and blocks CLOCK/BMAL1 (negative feedback). Second loop: REV-ERBα represses and RORα activates BMAL1 via ROR response elements (RRE). Clock-controlled genes (CCGs) include DBP, REV-ERBα, RORα. Casein kinase phosphorylates PERs, controlling nuclear import and degradation; time lags create oscillation.
- **Chrono-delivery / chronotherapy**: efficacy depends on time of administration (e.g., optimal chemotherapy timing; topical formulations more efficient at night).
- **Bidirectional aging–clock relationship**: clock dysfunction accelerates age-related disease; aging disrupts circadian behavior and physiology.

## Key Concepts
- **SCN master clock**: ~20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus, receives light cues via the retina; synchronizes peripheral clocks in nearly all cells. Human free-running rhythm is slightly longer than 24 h and resets on light exposure (jet lag needs a few days to readjust).
- **"Right molecule, right place, right amount, right time"** (paraphrasing Edmunds) — the principle of circadian organization.
- **Clock gene history**: PER first identified in fruit flies 1971; first mammalian clock gene Clock (Circadian locomotor output cycles kaput) in mice 1997; first human clock gene 2001 (Fu et al.). PER protein highest early evening, lowest early day. Gene family: period (per), clock (clk), cycle (cyc), timeless (tim) and others.
- **Desynchronization harms**: shift workers show more obesity/metabolic syndrome, elevated cortisol and BMI, cardiovascular risk; higher breast cancer with more night-shift years/hours; prostate cancer risk rises with long-haul flight hours (Nordic pilots) and rotating shifts (Japanese workers). Shift workers show altered promoter methylation of core circadian genes (top pathways include DNA repair/replication) — shift work recognized as a cancer risk factor.
- **PER1 and PER2**: described as tumor suppressor genes.
- **Clock-gene-deficient animals**: CLOCK deficiency reduces life span (and increases cataracts); BMAL1 deficiency → early aging and age-related pathologies; clock-deficient models show more frequent, severe dermatitis.
- **Skin clock genes**: shown expressed in human skin cells in 2000 (Zanello et al.).
- **Nighttime skin data**: TEWL ~25% higher at night than morning/afternoon; barrier recovery rate lowest between 8 and 11 p.m.; itching higher at night.
- **DNA repair timing**: alkyl guanine transferase (AGT) activity peaks at midnight; XPA expression peaks at night; epidermal proliferation ~30-fold higher at night than at noon.
- **UVB disrupts clocks**: NHEK exposed to 10 mJ/cm² UVB downregulate clock genes for up to ~20 hours (Kawara 2002); ATP production rhythm lost and ATP not recovered after 10 hours (Dong et al.). Sirtuins also follow a circadian pattern disrupted by UVB → more oxidative damage.
- **Epidermal stem cells and the clock**: stem cells protect themselves at peak light and divide at night; clock disruption → premature tissue aging and higher tumor predisposition; restoring rhythm increases long-term regenerative capacity (Janich/Benitah 2011).

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Night products: target repair/regeneration and leverage higher barrier permeability (penetration higher evening/night) — but also higher night TEWL and lowest barrier recovery 8–11 p.m., so support barrier in evening routines.
- Day products: emphasize protection (UV, antioxidant, environmental), since cells minimize proliferation and maximize defense by day.
- Protect clock synchrony: UVB even at low, non-cytotoxic doses (10 mJ/cm²) desynchronizes keratinocyte clock genes and energy rhythms — UV protection doubles as clock protection.
- Test design: synchronize cells (starvation then release) before measuring rhythmic endpoints (clock gene mRNA, ATP) over time; compare UV-exposed vs. unexposed.
- Claim angle: products that keep skin cell functions synchronized with circadian rhythm to maintain optimal function and slow aging; sirtuin and energy (ATP) support link to circadian claims.

## Mental Models
- Timing is an efficacy variable: the same active can perform differently by time of application.
- Clocks guard the genome: separating proliferation (night) from UV exposure (day) protects DNA; uncoupling clock from cell cycle → instability, aging, cancer.
- UV damage is also a scheduling problem: beyond direct DNA damage, it breaks the cell's timetable and energy cycle.
- Lifestyle desynchronizers (night work, travel, stress) are aging accelerators.

## Anti-patterns
- **Ignoring time of day in product design and testing**: misses optimal efficacy windows.
- **Treating low-dose UVB as harmless**: 10 mJ/cm² suppresses clock genes ~20 h and ATP rhythm >10 h.
- **Chronic circadian disruption (shift work, long-haul flying)**: linked to metabolic disease, cancers and accelerated aging.
