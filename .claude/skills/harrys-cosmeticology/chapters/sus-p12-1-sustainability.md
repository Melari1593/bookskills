# Part 12 (12.0–12.7): Sustainability and Eco-Responsibility — A Global Approach for the Cosmetic and Personal Care Industry
**Authors**: Alban Muller (editor's overview, 12.6, 12.7; Alban Muller Group); Jamie Pero Parker & Phil Watson (12.1, RTI International); Michael J. Balick (12.2, New York Botanical Garden); Geneviève Bridenne (12.3, Alban Muller Group); Jean-Marc Seigneuret (12.4, Alban Muller Group); Roberto Dal Toso (12.5, IRB SpA)

## Core Idea
Sustainability has become a business paradigm (people, planet, profit), driven by population growth (×4 in the last century, heading to 9 billion), finite resources, regulation and consumers who now judge company values. For cosmetics, that means two paths. (1) Improve existing practice: local cultivation, plant breeding, good agricultural practices, eco-responsible extraction, drying and formulation. (2) Adopt new technologies such as plant cell cultures and biofermentation. Both rely on transparency and collaboration, which are also the core of open innovation. The editor's thesis is that eco-design itself creates innovation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Triple bottom line (Elkington, 1998)**: measure success by people (cost to workers), planet (environmental price) and profit.
  - When to use: framing strategy, CSR reporting and supplier selection.
- **Open Innovation for Sustainability (OIS)** (12.1): open innovation (OI) brings in, and gives out, external ideas, IP and partners. OI and sustainability both need **transparency** and **collaboration**, so they reinforce each other.
  - When to use: sustainability problems too big for one firm (packaging, ingredient substitution, supply chain).
  - How: state innovation needs publicly or through intermediaries; partner with suppliers, government, consumers, NGOs and even competitors; out-license or donate green IP (WBCSD Eco-Patent Commons, Nike GreenXChange); spin out unused green tech.
- **Evolutionary model of sustainable innovation (Fig 1.1)**: narrow drivers (regulation, consumer demand, resource cost, waste) bring in suppliers, government and consumers → broader drivers (consumer demand turns into a requirement, a values-led workforce) lead to social auditing and society joining the ecosystem → full embrace (altruism, awareness of physical resource scarcity) creates a *superordinate goal*, so competitors and NGOs collaborate.
- **Six traits of OIS companies**: systemic, enterprise-level, committed (leadership rather than follower or compliance strategy), collaborative (more partners), invested (builds the whole supply chain, real triple bottom line), transparent (internal and external reporting).
- **Six-element ethnobotanical development approach (Balick, 12.2)**: (1) accurate identification under "good botanical practices"; (2) a clear purpose for each plant, in its most effective part and form, not as a marketing prop; (3) truthful claims about traditional use; (4) no environmental degradation from harvest; (5) no harm to local communities' access to the plant; (6) compensation to source communities and countries under the UN Convention on Biological Diversity.
- **Plant discovery strategies**: random survey (shotgun screening), phylogenetic survey (search relatives of an active species), ethnobotanically directed survey (traditional use → assay → active extract).
- **Peters' sustainable-harvest protocol for non-timber forest products (NTFPs)**: (1) choose species by ease of harvest and resilience (fruits are easier than roots; a single mass fruiting is easier to manage than sporadic fruiting); (2) forest inventory (productive plants/ha); (3) yield studies by plant size and habitat; (4) set sustainable harvest levels (no more than annual growth for stems/leaves; leave enough seed for fruits); (5) monitor regeneration, pollination and predation, and adjust harvest; replant or thin the canopy if needed.
- **Bank-account model of wild resources**: the population is the principal and the yield is the interest. Canes/leaves: spend all the interest. Fruits: spend only part and reinvest some. Never spend principal; adjust for "inflation" in poor years.
- **Three imperatives for plant-ingredient makers (12.4)**: grow biomass as close to the processing plant as possible; use plants richest in the useful molecules; meet all quality and safety requirements.
- **Five-level plant identification (12.4)**: scientific binomial with author (e.g., *Matricaria recutita* L.), plant part used, botanical (macro/microscopic) identification, phytochemical (TLC/HPLC/GC) identification with a pharmacopeia sampling plan, and genomic identification (PCR-sequenced short DNA, especially mitochondrial) for traceability and adulteration detection, pending a full database.
- **Plant breeding principles (12.4)**: mass selection (cheap, intuitive, traits drift) vs cross-breeding plus marker-assisted selection. Select for (1) yield and harvest frequency, (2) autonomy/disease resistance (fewer fungicides and inputs), (3) technological quality (highest active content, so less biomass to dry, ship and process, and a lower carbon footprint).
- **Eco-responsible extract chain (12.6)**: traced local cultivated plant → fast drying → cut "drug" → grind just before extraction → water/ethanol maceration → filtration → concentration with solvent recovery → low-energy drying (zeodration) → HPLC standardization on actives → microbial cleanliness (flash pasteurization) → glycerine carrier without preservatives.
- **Plant cell culture biotech (12.5)**: select a high-yield stable cell line (callus or suspension), cryopreserve it, elicit secondary metabolites, grow it in a sterile bioreactor, extract right after fermentation.

## Key Concepts
- **Barriers to sustainable innovation**: whole-life-cycle complexity; immature, costly green tech; regulatory uncertainty (when, what, changes, regional variation); fear of greenwashing accusations.
- **Path dependency**: firms look for ideas in familiar sources. OI looks across industries (e.g., hair care ingredient makers borrow from the carpet industry).
- **Transparency pressure**: EU regulations require toxicological documentation that small firms may lack, which pushes data sharing.
- **Nike example**: a US$6 million Environmental Apparel Design Tool was shared with the whole industry, because a tool used by one firm loses value.
- **Biodiversity numbers**: >300,000 flowering plant species; ~2,000 new species described each year; 70,000+ still undiscovered.
- **Brosimum alicastrum**: must produce >1.5 million seeds for one tree to reach reproduction, so heavy fruit harvest risks extinction.
- **Brazil nut (*Bertholletia excelsa*)**: ~200,000 harvesters, ~20,000 t/yr. Long-exploited populations fail to regenerate (Peres et al. 2003).
- **Marula (*Sclerocarya birrea*)**: social-governance case; best managed by combining statutory law with traditional custom.
- **Rosewood (*Aniba rosaeodora*)**: French Guiana distillers clear-felled trees within hauling distance; protection and replanting saved it.
- **CITES**: adopted in Washington, D.C. in 1973; 175 signatory countries; three appendices by threat level (prohibited, part-restricted, cultivated-only authorization).
- **Nagoya Protocol**: wild collection without access and benefit-sharing can lead to biopiracy lawsuits.
- **Palm oil**: cited for destruction of Indonesian and Malaysian rainforest.
- **Herboretum (12.3)**: association founded in 2004, a 9 ha open-air plant laboratory at St. Ay near Orléans (Loire Valley), >1,000 plant species (350 regional), 5 ecological gardens, 5 thematic gardens, 5 fauna observatories (Bird Island with >20 species, Butterfly Meadow in an observatory of 28 species, 10 beehives, Bat Cave with 8 species, Amphibian Riverside). The **Herboretum Network** was launched on 14 Nov 2011 in French Polynesia with first member GIMT (Monoï de Tahiti). Its charter covers knowledge sharing, local plant resources and awareness centers.
- **Lawn vs meadow**: a lawn has ≤5 plant types, a meadow up to 200. Clover fixes nitrogen and is not a weed.
- **Plant as micro-factory**: sunlight energy, CO2, N/P/K and trace elements as inputs. Chloroplasts (energy; fatty acids, flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenes), mitochondria (night ATP), ribosomes (proteins), dictyosomes (polysaccharides), ER (complex synthesis and transport), vacuoles (storage), nucleus ("hard drive").
- **Drivers of plant revival**: the move away from animal ingredients after BSE, distrust of overmedication, biodiversity awareness, science validating traditional uses, and sustainability.
- **Water content of fresh plants**: ~15% seeds, ~80% aerial parts, >90% fruit. Must be frozen or dried at once.
- **Drying target**: ≤12% water. Below this there is not enough free water for enzymes or microbes.
- **Transport limit**: aerial parts picked in late spring or summer should reach the dryer in <30 min. Seeds and underground parts tolerate longer transport.
- **Erysimum/sinigrin**: crushing tissue brings vacuolar sinigrin into contact with myrosinase, giving smelly or toxic breakdown products. Drying at 75°C fully stops myrosinase.
- **Mycotoxins**: aflatoxins and ochratoxins from damp storage. LD50 often < µg/kg. Analyze lipophilic-rich plants from hot, humid countries systematically.
- **PAHs**: toxic contaminants from fossil-fuel-fired dryers.
- **Phenylpropanoids (PP)**: derived from phenylalanine (nitrogen removed, catechols added). Plant roles: UV absorption, feeding deterrence, antimicrobial, flower and fruit color and scent, *Rhizobium* signaling. Highly concentrated in meristem cells.
- **Elicitors**: abiotic (UV, light, temperature, osmotic) and biotic (chitosan, methyl jasmonate, salicylic acid, microbes) switch cells to defense secondary metabolism. Cultured cells respond at low doses and short times because they lack cuticle and thick walls.
- **Cell culture track record**: >90 plant cell cultures listed as potential sources. Taxol, berberine, codeine, diosgenin, ginsenosides, morphine, scopolamine, vinblastine and vincristine have been produced. Production efficiency is the main barrier to scale-up. FAO endorses the technology.
- **Organic farming principles**: health, ecology, fairness, care. It avoids synthetic chemicals and GMOs and uses recycling and rotation. Its yield, quality and supply are unpredictable, making organic plants a "luxury raw material", and it does not guarantee freedom from environmental pollutants.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Source cultivated, local plants first. Wild collection is not sustainable and risks Nagoya biopiracy claims. Encourage local cultivation of "local biodiversity from elsewhere" (Herboretum Network).
- Use grower–manufacturer contracts to secure species identity, limit contamination and allow varietal selection.
- Good agricultural practices: clean seed; crop rotation and conservation agriculture; soil heavy-metal analysis (some plants accumulate metals); assess the ecological impact of non-native crops; agrochemicals only when absolutely necessary, at minimum dose, with a pre-harvest interval and a crop log; harvest timed to peak active content, not peak biomass; trained personnel.
- Wild picking: enough population density; no harm to plant or habitat (do not cut or unearth main roots; take bark from one side only in longitudinal strips); pickers who can tell look-alike species apart.
- Quality-check conventional-farm plants for identity, falsification, other plants, pesticides, heavy metals, mycotoxins, PAHs and origin-specific contaminants.
- Post-harvest: dry quickly to ≤12% water at a temperature balanced between speed and heat sensitivity (e.g., 75°C for erysimum). Sun drying speeds oxidation; solar-powered dryers are an option. Fresh-plant extraction (e.g., on-site hydrodistillation) must be immediate. Freezing keeps enzymes intact and costs carbon over long storage.
- Storage: control humidity and temperature; trap rodents and insects.
- Check the dried "drug" analytically before extraction to set the solvent/drug ratio (more plant means more solvent, evaporation and energy). Grind just before extraction to limit oxidation.
- Choose water or ethanol so the exhausted plant waste stays biodegradable. Distil and recycle solvent. Avoid glycol solvent-carriers that stay in the waste.
- Drying the extract: vacuum stoves (up to 48 h, degrade actives, sticky cake); spray drying (needs carrier); freeze-drying (high energy, refrigerant gases); zeodration (≤40°C, no carrier, water-only discharge, low energy) gives a "native extract".
- Standardize on active-molecule content by HPLC (history: TLC → UV → HPLC), not just the drug/solvent ratio, to support claims.
- Prefer flash pasteurization to added preservatives in extracts. Preservatives show up on the INCI list and worry consumers.
- Certification: organic plants have no different actives but lower environmental impact. Essential oils and floral waters certify easily (preservation is the hurdle for floral waters). Cold-pressed oils oxidize less. Extracts are complex (agro-source, solvent residue thresholds, carrier).
- GMO: medicinal plants are mostly rustic and unmodified. Check traceability for oils from industrial crops (wheat, sunflower, palm).
- Formulating "green": real naturals, not a chemical base plus 0.5% chamomile glycolic extract. Switch mineral to vegetable oils (now lower peroxide, colour and odour; cosmetic oils meet REACH, food oils do not). Use vegetable esters to replace silicones, rosemary for antioxidant protection, floral waters or infusions in the water phase, and salicylic acid as preservative.
- Plant cell cultures: secrete into the medium for simpler purification; suspension cultures are more GMP-compliant and scale better; cryopreserve elite lines against genetic/epigenetic drift; extract right after fermentation to avoid storage degradation.
- Industrial frame (12.7): ISO, HACCP, GMP, EU Cosmetic Directives, REACH; recyclable boxes, biodegradable inks, corn-starch packaging; recycle water. Example: Alban Muller (founded 1978) uses 100,000 L/day of water and returns treated effluent to 4,500 m² of restored wetland gardens (begun 2007; reeds, irises and lilies, 3 beehives, hedgerow corridors); amphibians, insects and birds returned.

## Mental Models
- **Potency = sustainability**: a higher active concentration in the plant cuts biomass, drying, transport, solvent and carbon at every step (chamomile: 3× less plant, 65% lower CO2).
- **Waste follows the solvent**: the extraction solvent decides whether tonnes of spent plant can be disposed of or recycled.
- **Give as well as take**: OIS leaders share IP so the whole industry looks sustainable and pressure on resources falls.
- **Market demand ≠ ecosystem capacity**: set harvests by population ecology, not by orders ("nature does not offer a free lunch").
- **Decouple supply from the field**: cell cultures remove climate, season, geography and pesticide variables, giving standardization, safety and availability.
- **Natural must match synthetic performance**: consumers will not give up pleasure or efficacy, which forces innovation in natural formulas.

## Anti-patterns
- **Greenwashing / cavalier "sustainable" claims**: more "sustainably produced" rainforest product is sold than produced, as happened with early organic food.
- **Plants as marketing props**: botanicals at token levels or in ineffective forms; cheapest organic extracts used only to reach label thresholds.
- **False traditional-use claims**: e.g., food plants in shampoos marketed as "centuries-old" use.
- **Overharvesting wild fruit or seed**: Brazil nut stands that do not regenerate; rosewood clear-felling.
- **Buying raw materials blind**: ignores extraction conditions (palm oil deforestation).
- **Shipping plants thousands of km**: only a small fraction ends up in the product.
- **Treating OI as crowdsourcing or one-way harvesting of ideas**: misses the OIS gains.
- **Chasing only low-hanging fruit**: bold new technologies are needed.
- **Sun drying or fossil-fired dryers**: oxidation, PAH contamination.
- **Grinding long before extraction**: exposes the plant to oxidation.
- **Using glycerine as a maceration solvent**: it is only a carrier.
