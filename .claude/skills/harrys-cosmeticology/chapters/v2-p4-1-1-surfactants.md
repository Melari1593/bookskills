# Part 4.1.1: Surfactants — Thoughtful, Pro-active Intervention at the Interface of Multiphase Dispersed Systems
**Authors**: J. Mark Chandler (ACT Solutions Corp)

## Core Idea
Surfactants (surface-active agents) are amphiphilic molecules that act at interfaces (water/air, water/oil, water/solid) and underpin nearly every cosmetic category: emulsifying, cleansing, foaming, solubilizing, conditioning, dispersing and lubricating. Because personal care products are luxury/experience goods, surfactant selection drives not only function but the aesthetic and emotional cues (feel, lather, clarity, scent) that determine repurchase.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four charge classes**: anionic (net negative), nonionic (no charge), cationic (net positive), amphoteric (charge depends on pH).
  - When to use: first-pass selection of a surfactant for a function.
  - How: anionics for foam/cleansing and some emulsions; nonionics for steric emulsions, solubilization, foam stabilization; cationics for conditioning; amphoterics for mildness and foam boosting with anionics.
- **Four emulsion stabilization mechanisms**: (1) charge/ionic, (2) nonionic/steric, (3) liquid crystalline lamellar, (4) polymeric. Seldom used alone.
  - When to use: designing any cream/lotion; choose by pH, electrolyte, oil polarity and aesthetics.
  - How: see Formulation guidance; combine mechanisms (e.g., polymers to boost high-temperature stability of anionic/nonionic/LC emulsions).
- **HLB system (Griffin, 1940s)**: HLB of a nonionic = weight % hydrophilic portion / 5 (Steareth-20: 75% hydrophilic → HLB 15). Higher HLB = more hydrophilic.
  - When to use: optimizing steric (nonionic) stabilization.
  - How: determine oil-phase required HLB, then blend low+high HLB emulsifiers arithmetically (equal parts HLB 15 + HLB 5 = 10). Works independently of phase-volume ratio, total surfactant level and chemistry type.
- **Required-HLB jar test**: blends of sorbitan oleate (HLB 4.3) and polysorbate 80 (HLB 14.9) at even HLB 6–14; 2 g blend + 20 g oil in a 50 mL capped glass jar, fill with water, hand-shake 20 times; least separation = required HLB; if two even values tie, test the odd value between.
- **Shampoo architecture**: anionic sulfate (6–12%) + amphoteric/betaine (~5%) + nonionic amide (~2%) + salt for viscosity.
- **Dispersant demand study**: titrate dispersant level; the optimum gives the lowest dispersion viscosity (both lower and higher levels raise viscosity).

## Key Concepts
- **Amphiphile**: molecule with hydrophilic polar head and hydrophobic tail; reduces interfacial tension.
- **Emulsion formation two steps**: droplet creation (mechanical energy aided by lower interfacial tension) then stabilization against aggregation, creaming/sedimentation, Ostwald ripening and coalescence; emulsions are kinetically stable but thermodynamically unstable (density differences, Van der Waals forces).
- **Charge stabilization**: ionic double layer gives electrostatic repulsion; almost exclusively O/W; soaps formed in situ by neutralizing stearic acid with NaOH or TEA; soap systems need pH > 7.
- **Steric stabilization**: nonionic film around droplets; most formulation work but most robust to low/high pH, high electrolyte, low/high internal phase, varied oil polarity.
- **Liquid crystalline lamellar phases**: bilayer structures resembling skin lipid bilayers, formed in water alone or around droplets; claimed wash-off resistance, better moisturization, reduced evaporation, better delivery of water-soluble actives; independent of HLB.
- **Polymeric stabilization**: builds zero-shear (rest) viscosity and a matrix between droplets, shear thinning; polymers are weakly interfacially active so benefit from low-mid HLB nonionics.
- **Cleaning mechanism**: wet substrate, lift soil (tails attach to soil, heads to water), emulsify and suspend to prevent redeposition; anionic+nonionic blends clean best.
- **Foam**: gas dispersed in small liquid volume; stability depends on interfacial elasticity; anionics give most foam; collapse via film drainage below a critical thickness (Marangoni effect). Foam has no physical cleansing role but is a key consumer cue.
- **Micellar solubilization (IUPAC)**: incorporation of a solubilizate into/on micelles; do not use "solubilizer" as a synonym for solvent. Nonionics are the best solubilizers.
- **CMC**: concentration where micelles (tails inward) begin to form.
- **Aggregation structures**: with rising concentration, cubic then hexagonal (and some lamellar) phases; modified by amphoterics, nonionics and salt to create shear-thinning viscosity.
- **Krafft temperature**: lipophilic emulsifiers heated above it in water form lamellar liquid crystals useful in creams.
- **Zwitterionic point**: narrow pH range where amphoterics behave as nonionics; cationic at low pH, anionic at high pH.
- **Conditioning**: hair/skin carry net negative charge; cationics adsorb; conditioning shampoos deposit cationic polymers on rinsing, aided by nonionics.
- **Betaines**: technically cationic (never anionic) but behave like amphoterics.
- **Alkyl amines**: not true cationics; protonated by acid, cationic below pH 7.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Creams/lotions: emulsifier 1–5%; mainly anionic or nonionic; watch barrier disruption with more hydrophilic surfactants.
- Soap-stabilized emulsions need pH > 7, so unusable with AHAs/BHAs; phosphate esters and cationics tolerate wider pH.
- Required HLB of an oil blend = sum of each oil's required HLB × its fraction of the oil phase; use a low + high HLB pair each a few units from the target.
- LC emulsifier process: add solid emulsifier and other waxes to water at 80°C, stir to uniform white, remove heat while stirring; add room-temperature oil any time during cooling; no HLB adjustment; add small hydrocolloid, no additional surfactants.
- Polymer-stabilized O/W: add low-to-mid HLB nonionic to cut mixing energy; use polymers to raise high-temperature stability of other systems.
- Shampoo: sulfate 6–12% + amphoteric ~5% + amide ~2% + salt; sulfate-free raises cost, viscosity, flow, clarity and foam challenges.
- Hand wash: sulfonates, lower cost/foam/viscosity; facial cleansers: milder anionics with more amphoteric/nonionic; body wash: high emollient levels.
- Hair conditioner: long-chain cationic + fatty alcohol (viscosity) + ethoxylated fatty alcohol (stability); <10% solids, often ~5%.
- Foundations are often water-in-silicone → silicone nonionic emulsifiers; disperse inorganic particles with low-HLB nonionics that bind the particle/coating and dissolve in the oil.
- Roll-on antiperspirant (low viscosity, low pH, very high electrolyte, low internal phase, polar oils/fragrance): low+high HLB ethoxylated fatty alcohol blends. Sticks use surfactants only for washability.
- Never formulate amphoterics at their zwitterionic point.
- Stabilize anionic/amphoteric foam with nonionics such as alkanolamides; blends give flash foam, volume and longevity.
- Add nonionics to anionic cleansers to solubilize fragrance clearly.

## Mental Models
- Cosmetics are luxury experiences: a product that works but separates, feels bad or does not foam fails commercially.
- Combine mechanisms: charge, steric, LC and polymer stabilization reinforce each other rather than competing.
- HLB is arithmetic: once the oil requirement is known, chemistry can be swapped freely for cost/compatibility at the same ratio.
- Viscosity in cleansers is phase behavior: tune micelle-to-hexagonal transitions with amphoterics, nonionics and salt.

## Anti-patterns
- **Soap emulsions with hydroxy acids**: soaps require pH > 7.
- **Formulating amphoterics at the zwitterionic point**: they lose ionic character.
- **Calling a solubilizer a solvent**: causes confusion; solubilization is micellar.
- **Adding extra surfactants to LC emulsions**: disrupts the lamellar system.
- **Using HLB for ionic surfactants**: values are of limited use.
- **Using DEA amides uncritically**: fallen out of favor for toxicological reasons.
