# Part 4.3.2: Peptides for Anti-Aging Skin Care
**Authors**: Howard Epstein, Ph.D. (EMD Chemicals)

## Core Idea
Peptides — chains of two or more amino acids linked by amide (peptide) bonds — have been cosmetic anti-aging actives since the early 1990s. Their bioactivity is set by amino-acid sequence and they work at very low concentrations, but they face skin proteases, off-target receptor binding and poor permeability. The formulator's core task is choosing or modifying a peptide (lipidation, side-chain/backbone changes, non-natural amino acids, cyclization) and a vehicle that delivers it intact to its skin target.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Functional peptide categories**: signal peptides (matrikines), antioxidant peptides, neuropeptides / neurotransmitter inhibitors, carrier peptides, enzyme-inhibitor peptides.
  - When to use: classifying a peptide claim and choosing the matching test (collagen synthesis, ROS, muscle contraction, wound healing, enzyme inhibition).
  - How: map mechanism → target (ECM-producing fibroblasts, SNARE/acetylcholine receptor, Cu²⁺ delivery, ACE).
- **Three stabilization/delivery strategies**: (1) modify side chains of single amino acids — non-natural functional groups restrict conformational flexibility and increase metabolic stability; (2) modify the peptide chain itself; (3) build with non-natural amino acids for stability plus extra biological benefit.
  - When to use: a native sequence degrades or does not penetrate.
  - How: e.g., add a palmitoyl (fatty acid) group to a water-soluble peptide so it can enter the lipophilic stratum corneum; increase polar amino acids for aqueous solubility.
- **Permeability determinants**: acid dissociation constant (pKa), molecular size, binding affinity, solubility, partition coefficient; plus compatibility and stability in the formula.

## Key Concepts
- **Peptide bond / amide bond**: carboxyl of one amino acid reacts with amino group of the next, releasing water. IUPAC gives no specific definition of "peptide."
- **Size terms**: dipeptide (2), tripeptide (3); oligopeptide <20 amino acids; polypeptide 50–100; above that, protein. Alternative scale cited: small peptides 2–5, medium 4–50, long 40–80; small proteins 70–120.
- **Sequence matters most**: after length, amino-acid order determines biological properties.
- **Solubility**: peptides rich in basic and acidic amino acids tend to dissolve in aqueous buffers at physiological pH ~7.4.
- **Cosmeceutical**: term coined by Albert Kligman >40 years ago; no such regulatory category exists at FDA (drug/cosmetic schism). Kligman later said he regretted proposing it.
- **ECM and dermis**: basement membrane rich in collagens, laminin, fibronectin, elastins, proteoglycans; 16 collagen types; dermal collagen is type I (80–85%) and type III (8–11%); collagens give tensile strength; integrins are the common cell-surface receptors.
- **Matrikines**: peptide fragments released by enzymatic breakdown of ECM proteins (collagen, elastin, decorin, laminins, fibronectin, tenascins); act as messengers in wound healing/repair. Two classes: natural matrikines (signal directly) and cryptic matrikines (need peptide-bond cleavage to signal).
- **Antioxidant paradox**: large antioxidant doses work in animal models, but human trials (vitamin E, recombinant SOD) mostly fail — because many antioxidants are also pro-oxidant, especially with transition metals such as Cu(II), and some never reach the site of radical generation (SOD is too large to cross cell membranes).
- **Neuropeptides**: skin and brain derive from the same tissue; stress-released neuropeptides regulate cutaneous immunity and repair and are linked to itch, hyperhidrosis, flushing, atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, etc.
- **Carrier peptides**: transport trace elements (copper, manganese) for wound healing and enzyme regulation. GHK (H-Gly-His-Lys-OH), identified in human plasma in the 1970s, binds Cu²⁺ strongly; copper is a cofactor for collagen/elastin enzymes.
- **Enzyme-inhibitor peptides**: several di-/tripeptides inhibit ACE (block angiotensin I → II and protect vasodilator bradykinin).
- **Safety**: peptides are generally not considered immunogenic and degrade to natural amino acids.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Pick peptides whose sequence matches the target; low concentrations (ppm range) can suffice.
- Increase stratum corneum penetration by lipidation (palmitoyl) or fatty-acid derivatives; increase water solubility with polar residues.
- Protect against skin proteases using D-amino acids (e.g., D-phenylalanine), non-natural residues (ACHA) and cyclic structures — cyclization also improves selectivity for the target integrin.
- RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp) sequences bind receptors that signal collagen renewal and wound healing.
- Check peptide compatibility and stability in the final vehicle; delivery to the target at the right time is the main formulation challenge.
- Avoid relying on large proteins (e.g., SOD) for intracellular effects — they don't cross membranes.
- Peptides support "naturally derived" and healthy-aging marketing claims.

## Mental Models
- **Sequence = function**: treat a peptide as a coded message; scrambling order changes or removes activity.
- **Mimic the body's repair signals**: matrikines imitate ECM breakdown fragments that tell fibroblasts to rebuild.
- **Delivery is the bottleneck**: efficacy depends on surviving proteases and crossing the stratum corneum, not on in-vitro potency alone.

## Anti-patterns
- **Formulating native, unmodified short peptides and expecting penetration**: limited permeability and protease degradation.
- **Assuming more antioxidant = more protection**: antioxidant paradox; pro-oxidant behaviour with Cu(II) and failure to reach the site.
- **Ignoring receptor selectivity**: peptides can cause undesired effects via other skin receptors.
