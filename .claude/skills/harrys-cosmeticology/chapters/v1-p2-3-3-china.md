# Part 2.3.3: Achieving Global Market Access – Focus on China
**Authors**: Zhongrui (Ray) Li (Toxicological Risk Assessor, Intertek)

## Core Idea
China regulates cosmetics under the 1990 Regulations Concerning the Hygiene Supervision over Cosmetics, with a definition and ingredient lists similar to the EU, but with a split into "special-use" and "non-special-use" products and pre-market registration (with mandatory animal testing in MOH-accredited labs) for all imported cosmetics. Safety assessment focuses on impurities, new ingredients outside the IECIC inventory face years-long approval, and claims, organic and nano positioning are tightly constrained. Patience with a slow, complex process is a prerequisite for entering this market.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Special-use vs non-special-use classification**:
  - When to use: first step of any China market plan.
  - How: special-use = hair growth, hair dye, perm, hair removal, breast shaping, fitness (slimming), deodorizing, spot removal, sun block. Non-special-use = hair care, nail care, skin care, perfume, makeup. Imported products of both types require CFDA registration; domestic non-special-use products only need provincial notification; oral care is outside both categories (national standards, e.g., GB 8372-2008 toothpaste), likely to move under CFDA.
- **Impurity-focused safety assessment**: required risk assessment emphasizes prohibited substances and toxic impurities.
  - How: provide test reports for typical impurities (e.g., phenol in phenoxyethanol; dioxane, methanol, acrylamide, NDELA) and calculate MoS for traces in the finished product.
- **Ingredient inventory gate (IECIC)**: formulate only with listed existing ingredients; anything else is "new".
  - How: check IECIC 2003/2011 and 2012 batches; respect MAC (maximum approved concentration) and MUCAP (maximum used concentration in approved products); exceeding MUCAP requires safety evidence for the finished product.
- **Chinese label "patch"**: design a Chinese sticker applied over the original label; warnings must not be covered; CFDA reviews claims on the patch only, not original-language text or websites.

## Key Concepts
- **Chinese cosmetic definition**: daily-use chemical products applied to body surface (skin, hair, nails, oral, lips) by smearing, spraying or similar, to clean, remove odour, protect skin, make up, increase beauty.
- **Hygienic Standard for Cosmetics (2007)**: carries prohibited/restricted lists similar to the EU.
- **CFDA registration**: required before marketing for imported cosmetics; documents in simplified Chinese plus original-language technical documents; testing can take months; registration can take months to years.
- **Animal testing**: mandatory for finished products, using rats, rabbits, guinea pigs in MOH-accredited labs; GPMT (banned in EU) is the only accepted sensitization method, LLNA not accepted; first accepted in vitro method: 3T3 Neutral Red Uptake Phototoxicity Test (February 2012).
- **EU interplay**: EU banned animal testing for products and ingredients as of March 2013 (Art. 18 of EC 1223/2009) but the ban concerns tests for that regulation; tests done for third-country requirements (e.g., China) should be documented transparently in the PIF and may not be considered a violation.
- **Claims review**: all performance claims reviewed by a CFDA committee during registration with substantiation (e.g., clinical reports). Products for pregnant or lactating women are not accepted.
- **Organic**: not recognized for cosmetics; under GB/T 19630 (AQSIQ) only products with ≥95% organic ingredients and official certification may use the "organic" mark; third-party organic certifications not accepted.
- **Cosmeceutical**: not recognized; drug-like products fall under the Drug Control Law.
- **Labeling standard**: GB 5296.3-2008; ingredients by Chinese INCI names (Catalogue of International Cosmetic Ingredients, MOH); apply for a Chinese name if missing. No 26-allergen labeling.
- **Nanomaterials**: no official CFDA opinion on use or labeling; not encouraged in infant/child products.
- **Infant/child guide (Oct 12, 2012)**: minimize number of ingredients, especially colours, fragrances and preservatives; prove need for each.
- **New ingredient**: any ingredient used in cosmetics for the first time in China (not on CFDA list) even if used in EU/US; defined in Guidelines for Registration and Evaluation of New Cosmetic Ingredients (2011). Since CFDA took over from MOH in 2008, only three new ingredients approved.
- **IECIC batches 2012**: May 1674, July 637, September 1356 substances (lists not exhaustive).

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Design formulas from the existing-ingredient inventory to avoid multi-year delays; check MAC/MUCAP for each ingredient.
- Specify and test impurities in raw materials (phenol, dioxane, methanol, acrylamide, NDELA) and compute MoS for trace levels.
- Budget for animal testing and unopened samples for animal/clinical testing during registration.
- Prepare claim substantiation dossiers; slimming, breast care and hair growth claims need effective-ingredient data and production process sketch.
- Avoid "organic" claims and maternity positioning; keep nano out of children's products.
- Registration dossier (imported): application form; formula with INCI names in English and Chinese; product specification (quality standard); MOH-accredited lab test report; manufacturing process description/chart (special use); original or China-designed package/label (and Chinese instructions for special use); free sale certificate from country of origin/manufacture; safety data/test reports for hazardous-ingredient impurities; BSE certificate; business license of the China responsible unit; authorization letter; agency certificate if needed; contract-manufacturing agreement or GMP/ISO certificate; unopened samples; other helpful documents.

## Mental Models
- In China the inventory is the formulator's palette: "not listed" effectively means "not usable" for the near term.
- EU safety thinking centres on intentional ingredients; Chinese assessment centres on impurities, so supply both views.
- Claims are approved, not just substantiated: anything outside the patch's reviewed claims is risky.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming EU/US approval transfers**: an ingredient used in the EU/US is still "new" in China if not on the list.
- **Relying on validated alternative methods**: CFDA generally rejects them (except 3T3 NRU phototox).
- **Marketing "organic" via third-party certification**: not accepted.
- **Covering warnings with the Chinese patch**: warnings must stay visible.
