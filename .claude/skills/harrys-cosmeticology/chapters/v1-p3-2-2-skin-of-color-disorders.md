# Part 3.2.2: Dermatologic Disorders in Skin of Color
**Authors**: Aanand N. Geria, MD

## Core Idea
Skin of color (enough pigment to impart a color other than white — African, Asian, Latin, Native American, Pacific Islander or mixed origin) is projected to describe the US majority by 2050. Pigmentary disorders — especially melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) — are among the top reasons these patients see a dermatologist. The chapter reviews melasma, PIH, vitiligo, idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis and pseudofolliculitis barbae, their mechanisms and treatment, stressing that sun protection is indispensable.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fitzpatrick skin phototypes**: classify by tanning ability and sunburn propensity; correlate with pigmentation (Type I typical of Celtic origin; Type VI of Sub-Saharan African ancestry).
  - When to use: anticipating pigmentary disorder risk (melasma peaks in III–IV).
- **Melasma clinical patterns**: centrofacial (most common: forehead, cheeks, nose, upper lip, chin), malar (cheeks, nose), mandibular (jawline); mixtures common.
  - When to use: diagnosis and documenting response.
- **Pigment depth assessment by Wood's lamp**: epidermal pigment responds better than dermal.
  - When to use: predicting treatment outcome and setting expectations.
- **Vitiligo types**: localized, generalized (most common; symmetric macules on fingers, wrists, axillae, groin, orifices), universal (≥80% skin involvement).
- **Vitiligo treatment goals**: halt progression, induce repigmentation, acceptable cosmetic result, address psychosocial impact.

## Key Concepts
- **Melasma**: acquired patchy hyperpigmentation from biologically hyperactive melanocytes; ~5 million affected in US; 10% of cases in men; prevalence 8.8% (Hispanic women, Southern US) to 40% (Southeast Asian females). Risk factors: family history (genetics), sun exposure (upregulates melanocyte-stimulating factors), estrogen (pregnancy, oral contraceptives).
- **PIH**: pigment increase after inflammation; extremely common in skin of color; causes include acne, eczema, psoriasis, burns, cosmetic procedures. Treat the underlying cause first, then as for melasma.
- **Vitiligo**: acquired depigmentation, 0.5–1% of population; nearly half present before age 20; no sex or race difference but more noticeable/devastating in darker skin. Histology: absent melanocytes. Most likely autoimmune in genetically predisposed (~20% have an affected first-degree relative). Associated with Addison's disease, pernicious anemia, diabetes, thyroid disease, alopecia areata. Koebnerization: lesions at trauma sites. Spontaneous repigmentation in ≤15–25%.
- **Idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis**: ~80% of people over 70; 1–6 mm well-defined hypopigmented flat smooth spots on forearms and shins; sun exposure implicated; more visible in dark skin.
- **Pseudofolliculitis barbae (PFB)**: razor bumps; newly shaved tightly curled hairs re-enter skin causing inflammation; common in beard of black men and groin of women; may lead to PIH and keloids.

## Formulation & Practical Guidance
- Sun protection (protective clothing + broad-spectrum sunblock) is critical; without it any hyperpigmentation regimen fails.
- Other hyperpigmentation options: laser therapy, chemical peels.
- Vitiligo: narrow-band UVB phototherapy is gold standard and most used; excimer laser; surgery for focal non-progressive cases failing phototherapy; cosmetic camouflage for face/neck.
- IGH: liquid nitrogen may help but risks post-inflammatory hyper- or hypopigmentation in dark skin.
- PFB: ideal is stop shaving, use trimmer leaving 1–2 mm stubble; else chemical depilatories, laser hair removal, eflornithine.
- Vitiligo response is slow and often modest — set expectations and address psychosocial impact; monobenzone total depigmentation only for extensive recalcitrant disease wanting uniform color.
- Melasma patients: screen for pregnancy, oral contraceptive use and family history; reinforce sun avoidance as estrogen and UV are key drivers.
- For PFB patients who must shave, combine anti-inflammatory topicals with hair-removal alternatives to prevent PIH and keloids.
- Cosmetic camouflage products are a legitimate tool for visible depigmentation on face and neck.
- Product development opportunity: improved understanding of pigmentation mechanisms should drive more effective medications and cosmeceuticals for ethnic skin.

## Mental Models
- **Fix the trigger before the stain**: PIH clears only once acne/eczema/inflammation is controlled.
- **Depth predicts response**: epidermal pigment is treatable; dermal is stubborn.
- **In dark skin, any injury is a pigment risk**: procedures (cryotherapy, peels) can create the very dyschromia being treated.

- **Visibility amplifies burden**: the same disorder (vitiligo, IGH) is no more frequent in dark skin but far more noticeable and distressing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Promising a vitiligo cure**: none exists; goals are halting progression and cosmetic acceptability.
- **Ignoring hormonal context in melasma**: pregnancy and oral contraceptives sustain estrogen-driven pigment.
- **Treating hyperpigmentation without sun protection**: regimen bound to fail.
- **Treating PIH without addressing the active cause**.
- **Aggressive cryotherapy for IGH in dark skin**: can cause new hyper/hypopigmentation.
- **Continuing close shaving in PFB**: perpetuates inflammation, PIH and keloids.
