# Patterns & Techniques

## Foodpairing by shared aroma
**When to use**: you want to justify or discover a non-obvious ingredient pairing.
**How**: identify each ingredient's key odorants (VOCs above their own odour recognition threshold); look for overlap in specific molecules (not just aroma type). A shared *specific molecule* (large-dot equivalent) is a much stronger signal than merely sharing an aroma *type*.
**Trade-offs**: shared aromas predict candidates worth tasting, not guaranteed success — molecular overlap is necessary but not sufficient; you still need to taste and adjust for taste/texture balance.

## Pairing-grid chaining
**When to use**: you want to build out a full recipe or explore an aroma neighborhood beyond one ingredient.
**How**: start from a main ingredient's pairing grid (up to 10 candidates); pick one or more candidates; open that candidate's own pairing grid as the next "main ingredient"; repeat. (Book example: quinoa → basil → chorizo.)
**Trade-offs**: each hop can drift further from the original ingredient's character — periodically check back against the original to avoid losing the thread.

## Flavour-flavour learning (evaluative conditioning)
**When to use**: introducing a disliked or unfamiliar ingredient to someone.
**How**: pair the disliked/unfamiliar ingredient with an ingredient the person already likes, ideally one carrying a strong reward cue (sweetness, fat, caffeine, alcohol); repeat exposure over time to transfer the liking.
**Trade-offs**: takes repeated exposure, not a single serving; works best when the liked ingredient's flavour is prominent enough to carry the disliked one without masking it entirely.

## Overtoning
**When to use**: you want to add depth/complexity to a dish without introducing jarring contrast.
**How**: combine multiple ingredients that all share strong aromatic links in the same aroma type(s) (e.g., chocolate + caramel + coffee, all roasted/caramellic/nutty).
**Trade-offs**: produces subtlety rather than excitement; if you want a more dynamic, memorable dish, deliberately contrasting ingredients (different profiles) may serve better, at the cost of needing more careful balancing (see Complexity Management below).

## Contrasting textures
**When to use**: any dish that risks feeling flat or one-note structurally, regardless of how well the aromas pair.
**How**: identify whether your ingredients are "soft" or "crunchy/crispy" (of ~60 texture types identified); ensure at least one representative from each group is present (chips+guacamole, fries+ketchup, mousse+crumble).
**Trade-offs**: none noted — this is treated as a near-universal requirement for an appealing dish, independent of aroma pairing quality.

## Contrasting tastes
**When to use**: balancing the overall flavour profile of a dish or drink.
**How**: include at least two of the five basic tastes (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami); use known counterbalancing relationships — salt reduces bitterness, salt balances sweetness, sourness cuts sweetness, umami/sweet balance each other, fat reduces perceived alcohol.
**Trade-offs**: over-relying on one balancing pair (e.g., always salt+sweet) can make dishes formulaic; vary which taste pairs you contrast.

## Complexity management
**When to use**: any time you're combining more than 2-3 ingredients or aroma-linked elements in one dish.
**How**: track (a) total distinct aroma molecules present, (b) how many different aroma types/descriptors are involved and whether they overlap, (c) how many contrasting taste/texture elements are present. Start with five or fewer ingredients; add complexity incrementally and taste at each step.
**Trade-offs**: too little complexity reads as boring; too much (especially many contrasting, non-overlapping profiles at once) overwhelms even a receptive diner — the relationship between complexity and liking is an inverted U, not a straight line, and shifts favourably with repeated exposure to the same dish.

## Building an aroma library (personal training method)
**When to use**: ongoing — a background practice for the cook/taster, not a per-dish technique.
**How**: deliberately and frequently smell/taste varied ingredients, starting with kitchen spices; name and track each; compare closely related items head-to-head (cinnamon vs. cloves; nutmeg vs. oregano vs. marjoram); take breaks to avoid olfactory fatigue; reset a fatigued nose via a neutral/strong smell (own arm, coffee bean) or a plain cracker.
**Trade-offs**: takes sustained practice over time; rushing sessions without recovery breaks degrades judgment reliability.
