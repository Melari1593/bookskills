## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The Foodpairing Hypothesis** (Ch 1): ingredients that pair well together tend to share key aroma molecules. This is a testable chemical claim, not a metaphor — discovered independently by a perfumer (jasmine/liver share indole) and chefs (kiwi/oyster share green aldehydes; white chocolate/caviar share aroma molecules). Shared aroma predicts *candidates worth tasting*, not guaranteed success — a chef's judgment on taste and texture still decides the final dish.

**Aroma is ~80-90% of flavour** (Ch 3): the tongue only detects five basic tastants (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami); nearly everything else you call "flavour" is olfaction, reaching the nose orthonasally (sniffing) or retronasally (via the throat while chewing/swallowing). Pinch your nose while eating and you lose almost all perceived flavour — proof the nose, not the mouth, does most of the work.

**Key odorants, not all VOCs, matter** (Ch 2): ~10,000 volatile organic compounds are known in food, but only those present above their own odour recognition threshold register as flavour (coffee has 1,000+ VOCs, only ~30-40 key odorants). Mixtures aren't additive: synergy (combined > sum), inhibition (one cancels another), suppression, and overshadowing all occur, often concentration-dependently. Beyond ~4 odorants a mixture is perceived as one new unified scent ("synthetic processing"); beyond ~20 evenly-spread odorants it trends toward generic "olfactory white."

**Aroma molecules form via four processes** (Ch 5): ripening (sugars → secondary metabolites), cutting/oxidation (unsaturated fats → new aldehydes, e.g. cucumber), heat (Maillard reaction at 140°C, caramelization at 160°C), and fermentation (sugars → alcohols/acids/esters). Ten chemical functional groups carry essentially all key food aromas: aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, esters, lactones, acids, terpenes/terpenoids, furans/furanoids, furanones, phenols (full table in [ch05](chapters/ch05-aroma-molecules.md)).

**The 14 Aroma Types & 70 Descriptors** (Ch 7) — Foodpairing's "language of scent," the classification every ingredient in the book is scored against:

| Type | Descriptors |
|---|---|
| Fruity | apple, banana, berry, coconut, fruity, grape, peach, pineapple, tropical |
| Citrus | citrus, grapefruit, lemon, orange |
| Floral | floral, geranium, honey, rose, violet |
| Green | cucumber, fatty, grass, green, oat flakes, waxy |
| Herbal | herbal, mint, thyme |
| Vegetable | bell pepper, cabbage, celery, garlic, mushroom, onion, potato |
| Caramel | caramel, maple |
| Roasted | coffee, earthy, fried, malty, popcorn, roasted |
| Nutty | hazelnut, nutty, tonka |
| Woody | balsamic, phenolic, pine, smoky, woody |
| Spicy | anise, camphor, cinnamon, clove, cumin, pungent, spicy, vanilla |
| Cheesy | acidic, buttery, cheesy, creamy |
| Animal | animal, fishy, meaty |
| Chemical | burnt, dusty, petroleum, soapy, solvent (off-odours — avoid, don't pair toward) |

An **aroma wheel** visualizes one ingredient's profile across these 14 types (inner ring) and their descriptor concentrations (outer ring, greyed out if absent). A **pairing grid** lists a main ingredient plus up to 10 candidates, with dot size distinguishing "aroma type present" (small dot) from "shares a specific molecule" (large dot — the strongest pairing signal). Pairing exploration chains: open a suggested pairing's own grid to keep discovering (e.g. quinoa → basil → chorizo).

**Aroma pairing alone doesn't make a good dish** (Ch 7): also contrast textures (≥1 soft + ≥1 crunchy/crispy, of ~60 identified types), contrast tastes (≥2 of the 5 basic tastes, using known counterbalances — salt reduces bitterness, sourness cuts sweetness), and manage complexity (start with ≤5 ingredients; liking rises with complexity only up to a point, then falls — an inverted U, not a straight line).

**Preferences are mostly learned, not innate** (Ch 4): flavour-flavour learning (evaluative conditioning) can shift a disliked ingredient toward liked by repeatedly pairing it with an already-liked, high-reward flavour (sweet, fatty, caffeinated). Aroma discrimination itself is trainable — see [ch06](chapters/ch06-building-aroma-library.md) for the practice method.

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the book's **conceptual/scientific framework only** (Introduction, pp. 7–37): the foodpairing hypothesis, aroma chemistry, the 14-type classification system, and dish-building principles.

**Not included**: the book's 91-ingredient pairing database (pp. 40–371, "Ingredients & Pairings") — individual aroma-wheel profiles and top-10 pairing grids for ingredients like kiwi, chocolate, truffle, coffee, oyster, etc., plus 750+ secondary pairing entries for related dishes/preparations. This was deliberately excluded from extraction as a large raw reference database rather than transferable methodology; consult the physical/digital book directly for a specific ingredient's actual pairing data, or apply the frameworks above (Ch 5 aroma-molecule chemistry + Ch 7's 14-type system) to reason about any ingredient's likely profile from first principles.

Also not included: the UK/US terminology glossary (p. 387) and citation endnotes (p. 387) — minor reference material with low reuse value as a skill.
