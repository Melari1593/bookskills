# Cheatsheet

## The 14 Aroma Types (decision reference)

| Type | Descriptors | Reach for this ingredient when you want... |
|---|---|---|
| Fruity | apple, banana, berry, coconut, fruity, grape, peach, pineapple, tropical | Bright, sweet lift |
| Citrus | citrus, grapefruit, lemon, orange | Acidity + aromatic lift, cuts richness |
| Floral | floral, geranium, honey, rose, violet | Perfumed top note, elegance |
| Green | cucumber, fatty, grass, green, oat flakes, waxy | Freshness, raw/crisp character |
| Herbal | herbal, mint, thyme | Cooling, aromatic freshness |
| Vegetable | bell pepper, cabbage, celery, garlic, mushroom, onion, potato | Savoury base, umami-adjacent depth |
| Caramel | caramel, maple | Sweet depth without fruitiness |
| Roasted | coffee, earthy, fried, malty, popcorn, roasted | Maillard depth, bitterness-adjacent complexity |
| Nutty | hazelnut, nutty, tonka | Richness, warmth, roundness |
| Woody | balsamic, phenolic, pine, smoky, woody | Smoky/resinous backbone |
| Spicy | anise, camphor, cinnamon, clove, cumin, pungent, spicy, vanilla | Warmth, heat-adjacent complexity |
| Cheesy | acidic, buttery, cheesy, creamy | Richness, tang, umami |
| Animal | animal, fishy, meaty | Savoury depth, umami intensity (use sparingly) |
| Chemical | burnt, dusty, petroleum, soapy, solvent | Avoid — these are off-note/spoilage indicators, not target flavours |

## Decision rules

- **If two ingredients share a large-dot (specific molecule) link on a pairing grid** → prioritize this pairing over one that merely shares an aroma-type (small dot) with no confirmed shared molecule.
- **If a dish feels aromatically "matched" but still flat** → check texture and taste balance before adding more aroma-linked ingredients; the aroma is not the whole dish.
- **If you want to introduce an ingredient someone dislikes** → pair it with something they already like that carries a strong reward cue (sweet, fatty, caffeinated, alcoholic); repeat exposure, don't rely on one serving.
- **If a dish has 3+ contrasting (non-overlapping-profile) ingredients** → add at least one soft AND one crunchy/crispy element, and at least two of the five basic tastes, to keep it balanced rather than chaotic.
- **If you're unsure how much complexity to add** → default to five or fewer ingredients; complexity beyond that needs deliberate structure (shared aroma links = "overtoning") or it risks reading as overcomplicated.
- **If an aroma seems present but weak/undetectable in the final dish** → check the matrix (water/fat/alcohol) and temperature; hydrophobic aromas need fat or alcohol to register, hydrophilic aromas need water; heat drives volatile release.
- **If you want an aroma to persist longer in a dish or drink** → favor ingredients whose dominant aroma molecules have longer carbon chains (more persistent, e.g. dodecanal vs. propanal); shorter chains fade fast.
- **If two people disagree about how "complex" a dish is** → expect this; perceived complexity is subjective and shaped by frame of reference and repeated exposure, not an objective property of the dish alone.

## Thresholds & defaults

- **Synthetic-processing threshold**: beyond ~4 odorants, a mixture stops being perceived as separate components and becomes one unified new scent.
- **Olfactory-white threshold**: beyond ~20 evenly-spread odorants, mixtures trend toward a generic smell regardless of composition.
- **Starting ingredient count**: ≤5 ingredients recommended while learning to balance a dish.
- **Basic tastes to include**: at least 2 of {sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami} per dish for taste contrast.
- **Texture groups to include**: at least 1 "soft" + 1 "crunchy/crispy" element (of ~60 identified texture types).
- **Supertaster prevalence**: ~25% of people; non-tasters ~25%; remainder average sensitivity.
- **Flavour perception split**: ~80–90% olfaction (smell), ~10–20% gustation (taste) + trigeminal effects.

## Tells & smells (fast heuristics)

- Smells "chemical" (burnt, dusty, petroleum, soapy, solvent) → likely a storage/packaging off-odour, not a target flavour; treat as a defect signal, not a pairing opportunity.
- An ingredient's raw aroma seems faint or absent → it may need damage (cutting/bruising) or heat (Maillard/caramelization) to develop its characteristic aroma (common with vegetables).
- A dish "tastes bitter and not much else" when eaten with a blocked nose → confirms flavour perception is aroma-driven; re-test with nose unblocked before concluding a recipe lacks character.
- Two ingredients seem like an odd pairing but a shared molecule is documented (e.g. kiwi+oyster, chocolate+caviar, jasmine+liver) → treat as a genuine candidate worth testing, not a mistake.
