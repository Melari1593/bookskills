# Chapter 1: The Story of Foodpairing

## Core Idea
Ingredients that pair well together tend to share key aroma molecules — a discovery made independently by a perfumer (François Benzi, 1992: jasmine and liver both contain indole) and a chef (Heston Blumenthal, with white chocolate and caviar), then formalized by Bernard Lahousse and chef Sang-Hoon Degeimbre through GC-MS analysis of kiwi and oyster.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Foodpairing Hypothesis**: "Ingredients that pair well together share key aroma molecules." This is the founding, falsifiable claim behind the entire method — not a metaphor, a chemically testable statement.
  - When to use: as the starting hypothesis whenever you want to justify or discover a non-obvious pairing.
  - How: analyze both ingredients' volatile compounds (historically via GC-MS); look for overlapping compounds present above their odour recognition threshold.

## Key Concepts
- **GC-MS (gas chromatography–mass spectrometry)**: lab technique that separates and identifies the volatile compounds in an ingredient.
- **Aromatic link**: a shared aroma molecule (or molecule family) between two otherwise unrelated ingredients that explains why they taste good together.
- **Kiwître**: the founding dish (chef Sang-Hoon Degeimbre) — raw oyster over diced kiwi with croutons and lime-coconut cream — built on the discovery that kiwi and oysters share green, marine-like aldehydes.

## Mental Models
- Treat "weird" pairings as data, not error: an unusual-seeming combination (chocolate + caviar, jasmine + liver) is a signal to go looking for a shared aroma compound, not a reason to dismiss it.
- Intuitive pairing is real but bounded: chefs and home cooks pair well within familiar/cultural combinations by instinct, but instinct doesn't extend to genuinely novel combinations — that's where aroma analysis adds value instinct alone can't reach.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating foodpairing as a replacement for a chef's palate**: Heston Blumenthal is explicit that flavour pairing is "a wonderful tool for creativity, but only when used in concert with a chef's intuition, imagination and emotion." Shared aroma molecules predict *candidates* to try, not guaranteed successes — you still have to taste.
- **Assuming shared aromas always mean success**: even when two ingredients share many compounds, whether they'll actually combine well "is far from guaranteed" (Blumenthal) — molecular complexity means overlap is necessary but not sufficient.

## Worked Example
Sang-Hoon Degeimbre asked Bernard Lahousse in 2005: "why is it that when I smell kiwis, I also smell the sea?" Lahousse and bioengineer Jeroen Lammertyn ran a GC-MS aroma analysis and found that kiwi — beyond its fruity esters — contains green, grassy, fatty-scented aldehydes with a marine-like character similar to oysters and shellfish. That shared aldehyde profile became the *kiwître*: raw oyster served over diced kiwi, croutons, and lime-infused coconut cream — now a signature dish at L'Air du Temps. The same underlying logic (chocolate/caviar share aroma molecules per Benzi's later analysis for Blumenthal) generalizes: identify shared volatiles, then let a chef's technique turn the chemical link into an actual dish.

## Key Takeaways
1. The core hypothesis — shared key aromas predict good pairings — is chemically testable, not just intuition dressed up.
2. Foodpairing was discovered twice, independently, by a perfumer analyzing fragrance and a chef experimenting in the kitchen — the pattern shows up wherever someone compares aroma profiles systematically.
3. A successful "unconventional" pairing (kiwi+oyster, chocolate+caviar) is evidence the hypothesis works, but the chef's technique and judgment still make or break the final dish.
4. Since 2007, Foodpairing has catalogued over three thousand ingredients into the largest flavour database in the world, expanding the hypothesis from anecdote to a searchable system.
