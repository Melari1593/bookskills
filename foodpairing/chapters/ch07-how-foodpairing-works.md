# Chapter 7: How Foodpairing Works

## Core Idea
Foodpairing's proprietary system classifies every ingredient's aroma profile into 14 aroma types (further split into 70 descriptors), visualizes it as an aroma wheel, and generates pairing grids of candidate ingredients that share key aroma molecules — but a genuinely good dish still requires balancing complementary aromas against contrasting tastes and textures, and keeping overall complexity within what a diner can enjoyably process.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 14 Aroma Types & 70 Descriptors**: Foodpairing's classification "language of scent." Every ingredient's profile is expressed as presence/concentration across these 14 categories (see Reference Table below for full descriptor lists).
  - When to use: as the universal vocabulary for comparing any two ingredients' aroma profiles, replacing vague description with a structured, comparable classification.
  - How: an ingredient is analyzed for its VOCs; each key odorant is assigned to one of 70 descriptors, which roll up into one of the 14 types; the ingredient's full profile is the resulting set of types/descriptors and their relative concentrations.
- **Aroma wheel**: a two-ring circular visualization of one ingredient's profile — the inner ring shows all 14 aroma types (greyed out if absent), the outer ring shows the concentration of individual descriptors as coloured bands (distance from center = concentration; band thickness/length = relative amount).
  - When to use: to visually compare the "shape" of two ingredients' aroma profiles at a glance.
  - How: read clockwise around the 14-type inner ring; note which types have outer-ring bands (present) vs. none (absent, greyed out); longer/thicker bands = higher concentration of that descriptor.
- **Pairing grid**: a table with the main ingredient in bold and up to ten candidate pairings below it, one dotted column per aroma type; dot size encodes whether that type is present (small dot) or shares a specific aroma molecule with the main ingredient (large dot).
  - When to use: to generate concrete pairing ideas for an ingredient, then chain outward (look up one of the ten suggestions as a new "main ingredient" to keep exploring — the book's core exploration technique).
  - How: pick a main ingredient's grid; a large dot in a column = a real shared-molecule aromatic link on that aroma type, not just co-presence; select 1+ suggested pairings; optionally look up the pairing grid for one of those suggestions to extend the chain (e.g., quinoa → basil → chorizo).
- **From Pairing Ingredients to Delicious Dishes**: aroma-sharing alone doesn't make a dish — three additional levers determine whether a set of paired ingredients becomes an actually good dish.
  - **Contrast textures**: the book's team identified 60 distinct texture types across two groups (soft; crunchy/crispy) — include at least one contrasting texture from each group per dish (e.g., chips+guacamole, French fries+ketchup, mousse+crumble).
  - **Contrast tastes**: incorporate at least two of the five basic tastes (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami) so they can balance one another (salt reduces bitterness and balances sweetness; sourness can reduce perceived sweetness); also account for trigeminal effects (heat, cooling, astringency, fizz, alcohol burn).
  - **Manage complexity**: perceived liking rises with complexity only up to a point, then falls once a dish feels overcomplicated (an inverted-U relationship, stronger after repeated exposure than on first taste). Start with five or fewer ingredients while learning to balance a dish.

## Key Concepts
- **Aroma descriptor**: the most granular label (e.g., "pineapple," "cheesy," "smoky") — a molecule's base scent; 70 total, grouped into the 14 types.
- **Overtoning**: combining several ingredients that share strong aromatic links (e.g., chocolate, caramel, coffee — all roasted/caramellic/nutty) to build subtle depth without adding contrasting elements; contrasted with adding genuinely different-profile ingredients (almond, basil) which increases complexity and requires more careful balancing.
- **Unity-in-variety** (Daniel Berlyne): the aesthetic principle that the most successful pairings balance genuine variety against an underlying coherence — too much sameness is boring, too much unrelated variety is chaotic; skilled use of shared aroma types is what keeps variety "unified."
- **Related aroma profile**: the book's convention of also profiling a close botanical/culinary relative of the main ingredient (e.g., kiwi berry alongside kiwi) since it shares much, but not all, of the same profile.

## Mental Models
- Read an aroma wheel like a fingerprint, not a score: the goal isn't to compare "how big" one ingredient's wheel is overall, but which specific types/descriptors are shared with a candidate pairing partner.
- Chain, don't dead-end: every pairing grid entry is itself a valid new starting ingredient — treat pairing exploration as a graph traversal (main ingredient → suggested pairing → that pairing's own suggestions), not a single lookup.
- A large dot beats many small dots: a single aroma type where the main ingredient and a candidate share an actual specific molecule (large dot) is a stronger, more specific pairing signal than several aroma types both simply "being present" (small dots) without a demonstrated shared molecule.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stopping at aroma-sharing and skipping taste/texture balance**: a technically well-matched pairing (by aroma) can still be a flat or boring dish if it lacks textural contrast or a balancing taste — aroma pairing is necessary but not sufficient for a good dish.
- **Overloading a dish once pairings feel exciting**: adding every aromatically-linked ingredient you find (5+ contrasting elements) tends to overcomplicate a dish past the point where diners enjoy it — cap experimentation, especially early on, at around five ingredients.
- **Confusing "small dot" (type present) with "large dot" (shared specific molecule)**: treating every colored dot in a pairing grid as equally strong evidence overstates weak pairings and understates strong ones.

## Reference Tables

### The 14 Aroma Types and their descriptors

| Aroma type | Descriptors | Character/chemistry note |
|---|---|---|
| Fruity | apple, banana, berry, coconut, fruity, grape, peach, pineapple, tropical | Esters; lactones can add peach/coconut notes |
| Citrus | citrus, grapefruit, lemon, orange | Found in citrus fruits, coriander seed, lemongrass, lemon balm |
| Floral | floral, geranium, honey, rose, violet | Beta-damascenone, beta-ionone, (Z)-1,5-octadien-3-one |
| Green | cucumber, fatty, grass, green, oat flakes, waxy | Ranges cucumber-ish/fatty to grassy/waxy; milled grains, seaweed |
| Herbal | herbal, mint, thyme | Menthol, thymol |
| Vegetable | bell pepper, cabbage, celery, garlic, mushroom, onion, potato | Pyrazines, 1-octen-3-one, methanethiol; alliums/brassicas |
| Caramel | caramel, maple | Furaneol, maltol, sotolon |
| Roasted | coffee, earthy, fried, malty, popcorn, roasted | Maillard-reaction products; pyrazines, geosmin |
| Nutty | hazelnut, nutty, tonka | Benzaldehyde (almond), coumarin (tonka), ketones (hazelnut) |
| Woody | balsamic, phenolic, pine, smoky, woody | Terpenes/pinenes; smoking/cold-smoking imparts phenolics |
| Spicy | anise, camphor, cinnamon, clove, cumin, pungent, spicy, vanilla | Cinnamaldehyde, cuminaldehyde, eugenol, vanillin, camphor, estragole |
| Cheesy | acidic, buttery, cheesy, creamy | Cream, butter, ripe cheese, fermented dairy, vinegar |
| Animal | animal, fishy, meaty | Indole (liver — faecal/earthy/phenolic/perfumy/floral), skatole |
| Chemical | burnt, dusty, petroleum, soapy, solvent | Off-odours from storage/packaging issues |

### How to read a pairing grid (worked interpretation)
| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| No dot in a column | That aroma type is absent from the ingredient |
| Small colored dot | Aroma type present, but no confirmed shared molecule with the main ingredient |
| Large colored dot | Main ingredient and this pairing share a specific aroma molecule for that type — the strongest pairing signal |

## Worked Example
The book's cooked-quinoa pairing grid: quinoa (main, bold) shows dots for fruity, citrus, floral, green, herbal, vegetable, caramel, roasted, nutty, woody, spicy, and cheesy — but none for animal or chemical. Below it, ten candidates are listed (tomato, basil, walnut, beef ribeye steak, apricot, baked brill, grated horseradish, aji amarillo chilli, crab meat, pan-fried okra), each with its own dot row for comparison. Reading the grid: tomato shares large dots with quinoa on citrus and vegetable (confirmed shared molecules) plus five other types present in both — making it the strongest, most-linked candidate on the list. Walnut, by contrast, is the one candidate lacking a fruity dot entirely — flagging it as the aromatic outlier in this set. Following the book's own procedure, you could combine quinoa with tomato, basil, crab meat, and apricot into one dish (a quinoa summer salad), then continue the chain by opening basil's own pairing grid to find a further ingredient (e.g., chorizo) to extend the dish.

## Key Takeaways
1. Every ingredient in the Foodpairing system is classified across 14 aroma types and 70 descriptors — a fixed, comparable vocabulary rather than free-form tasting notes.
2. Aroma wheels visualize a single ingredient's profile; pairing grids compare a main ingredient against up to ten candidates, with dot size distinguishing "type present" from "shares a specific molecule."
3. Pairing exploration is a chain, not a single lookup: each suggested pairing has its own grid you can open next.
4. A good dish requires more than aroma overlap: deliberately contrast textures (soft vs. crunchy) and tastes (at least two of sweet/salty/sour/bitter/umami), and keep total ingredient complexity manageable (start ≤5 ingredients).
5. "Unity-in-variety" — enough shared aromatic structure to feel coherent, enough genuine difference to stay interesting — is the aesthetic target, not maximum novelty or maximum similarity.
